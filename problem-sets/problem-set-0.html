Problem Set 0

Show your work and explain your reasoning. Use the notation introduced in the relevant passus.

1. A simple grouping system

In a base-4 simple grouping system, the values \(1\), \(4\), \(4^2\), and \(4^3\) are represented by ●, ▲, ■, and ◆, respectively.

  1. What number is represented by ◆◆■●?

  2. What number is represented by ◆■■■▲?

  3. Express \(81\) in this system.

  4. Express \(210\) in this system.

2. Inventing a base-11 system

Create eleven distinct symbols of your own for the digit values \(0,1,2,\ldots,10\) in a base-11 positional system. Your symbols may be letters, drawings, or any other symbols you choose. State clearly which value each symbol represents.

  1. Give three examples of numbers in your system and translate each number into our usual base-10 system.

  2. Write a number in your base-11 system that uses at least three different symbols. Convert that number into base 5.

3. Multiplication in base 6

Construct a multiplication table in base 6. Include the product \(a\cdot b\) for each

\[ a,b\in\{(1)_6,(2)_6,(3)_6,(4)_6,(5)_6,(10)_6\}, \]

and write every product in base 6.

4. Babylonian notation

Babylonians used a base-60 positional system, but they wrote the numbers from 1 through 59 using only two basic signs: a vertical wedge for one and a horizontal wedge for ten. For much of their history they had no consistent symbol for zero, and spacing between groups could be ambiguous.

  1. Interpret the following sequence in at least four different ways:

horizontal wedge, vertical wedge, vertical wedge, vertical wedge

For each interpretation, use the notation introduced in Passus 2 and give its value in base 10.

  1. Write \(120\frac{1}{3}\) in base 60. Use a semicolon as the sexagesimal point.

6. Unit fractions

Show that, for every positive integer \(n\),

\[ \frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}. \]

Then use the identity to write \(\frac{1}{101}\) as a sum of unit fractions.

7. A second decomposition

Extra credit. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus includes

\[ \frac{2}{101}=\frac{1}{101}+\frac{1}{202}+\frac{1}{303}+\frac{1}{606}. \]

Find a different representation of \(\frac{2}{101}\) as a sum of distinct unit fractions.

8. Adding fractions in The Nine Chapters

The following method for adding fractions appears in The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art:

Multiply each numerator by the denominators of the other fractions. Add the resulting numbers to obtain the dividend. Multiply the denominators to obtain the divisor. Divide; if there is a remainder, use it as the numerator and the divisor as the denominator.

Using modern notation, give a complete explanation of what this procedure does and why it works.

9. “Seven has no inverse”

Some Babylonian texts say that “seven has no inverse.” What do you think they meant? Explain your answer in the context of Babylonian base-60 computation.

10. A quantity from the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus

Problem 32 asks:

A quantity, its one-third, and its one-fourth, added together, become 2. What is the quantity?

Solve the problem and explain your method.

11. Egyptian multiplication

  1. Use the Egyptian method of multiplication to multiply \(26\) by \(31\).

  2. Choose one additional pair of two-digit numbers and multiply them using the Egyptian method.

  3. Verify both answers using ordinary multiplication.

  4. Explain why the Egyptian method will always work.

12. Egyptian division

Ancient Egyptians also had a method for dividing integers that was similar to their multiplication method. To calculate \(132\div6\), one asks what must be multiplied by 6 to obtain 132. Perform this division using the Egyptian method and show your work.

13. Empty places on a Chinese counting board

Chinese counting rods used an empty position to represent a missing place.

  1. Draw or clearly describe how \(205\) and \(25\) would be distinguished on a counting board.

  2. Compare this with Babylonian notation. Why was an empty position less ambiguous on a counting board than in a written line of cuneiform numerals?
