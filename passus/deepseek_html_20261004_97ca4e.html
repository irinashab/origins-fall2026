1. Counting primes: the function π(N)

We already know what prime and composite numbers are. The natural next question is not whether primes are infinite — Euclid settled that — but how many there are up to a given bound, and how that number grows.

Define the prime-counting function

\[ \pi(N) = \text{the number of primes less than or equal to } N. \]

Here \( \pi \) names a function; it is not the circle constant. The same symbol is used for two unrelated things, and context tells them apart: in this lecture, \( \pi(N) \) always means a prime count. For example, \( \pi(10) = 4 \) because the primes at most \( 10 \) are \( 2, 3, 5, \) and \( 7 \). Notice that

\[ \pi(10) = 4, \qquad \pi(11) = 5, \qquad \pi(12) = 5. \]

The function increases by \( 1 \) whenever we reach a prime and stays unchanged at every other integer. Its graph is a staircase with unevenly spaced steps — each step marks a prime, and the gaps between steps grow irregular as \( N \) increases.

2. The Prime Number Theorem

Individual primes are difficult to predict: no formula produces the n-th prime, and the gaps between consecutive primes fluctuate unpredictably. Yet the overall number of primes up to \( N \) follows a remarkably regular law.

Prime Number Theorem. As \( N \) grows without bound,

\[ \pi(N) \sim \frac{N}{\log N}. \]

Here \( \log N \) is the natural logarithm, the logarithm to base \( e \), where \( e \approx 2.71828 \). Equivalently, \( \log N \) is the number \( t \) for which \( e^t = N \). Natural logarithms appear throughout the analytic theory of primes; the base is not a matter of convenience, but is forced by the calculus and complex analysis that underlie the proof.

The symbol \( \sim \), read “is asymptotic to,” means that the ratio of the two sides approaches \( 1 \):

\[ \frac{\pi(N)}{N / \log N} \to 1 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty. \]

In other words, for large \( N \) the estimate \( N / \log N \) is relatively accurate: the ratio \( \pi(N) / (N / \log N) \) gets closer and closer to \( 1 \), even though the absolute difference \( \pi(N) - N / \log N \) may continue to grow.

The theorem concerns relative error. It does not say that the difference between the count and the estimate approaches zero — in the table, that difference actually grows. What shrinks is the ratio: the last column drifts toward \( 1 \) as \( N \) grows.

Dividing both sides of \( \pi(N) \sim N / \log N \) by \( N \) gives \( \pi(N)/N \sim 1/\log N \). The left side is the proportion of integers from \( 1 \) to \( N \) that are prime. Since \( \log N \) grows without bound, that proportion tends to zero. There are infinitely many primes, yet they occupy a decreasing proportion of the integers. Primes become rarer as we go.

Jacques Hadamard and Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin independently proved the theorem in 1896, using ideas connected with Riemann’s zeta function. That function — and the question about its zeros that Riemann posed and that remains open — is the subject of the final sections of this passus. [1]

3. Divisibility, remainders, and congruence

Recall that \( d \mid a \) means that \( a = dk \) for some integer \( k \). Fix a positive integer \( m \), called the modulus. Two integers \( a \) and \( b \) are congruent modulo \( m \) if they have the same remainder upon division by \( m \); equivalently, if \( m \) divides their difference. We write

\[ a \equiv b \pmod{m}. \]

For example, \( 17 \equiv 2 \pmod{5} \), because \( 17 - 2 = 15 \) is divisible by \( 5 \). Both \( 17 \) and \( 2 \) leave remainder \( 2 \) when divided by \( 5 \).

The word modulo is the ablative form of the Latin modulus, a diminutive of modus (“measure”). It means roughly “by the measure of.” Gauss introduced it in the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) as part of the notation \( a \equiv b \pmod{m} \), where \( m \) is the modulus — the standard against which the two numbers are being compared. Earlier mathematicians (Fermat, Euler, Lagrange) worked with the same ideas, but phrased them as statements about divisibility of differences; Gauss made the remainder itself the central object and gave the whole theory its now-familiar vocabulary.

Every integer belongs to exactly one of the \( m \) remainder classes \( 0, 1, \ldots, m-1 \). Congruences can be added, subtracted, and multiplied: if \( a \equiv b \pmod{m} \) and \( c \equiv d \pmod{m} \), then

\[ a + c \equiv b + d \pmod{m}, \qquad ac \equiv bd \pmod{m}. \]

These remainder classes, with addition modulo \( m \), form what mathematicians call a group: a set with an operation that is closed, associative, has an identity, and gives every element an inverse. Here the identity is \( 0 \), and the inverse of a class is the class that brings it back to \( 0 \). Because the operation is also commutative, this is an abelian group.

This is what makes congruences useful. Large numbers can be replaced by their small remainders at every step of a computation. Since \( 17 \equiv 2 \pmod{5} \), we get \( 17^2 \equiv 2^2 \equiv 4 \pmod{5} \) without ever computing \( 289 \). Repeated squaring then lets us study enormous powers through small remainders — a technique we will use repeatedly below.

Two positive integers are coprime if their greatest common divisor is \( 1 \). For example, \( 8 \) and \( 15 \) are coprime even though neither is prime: \( 8 = 2^3 \) and \( 15 = 3 \cdot 5 \) share no prime factor. Coprimality, not primeness, is the condition that makes the Chinese Remainder Theorem work in §5.

4. The pigeonhole principle

Pigeonhole principle. If more than \( r \) objects are placed into \( r \) boxes, where \( r \) is a positive integer, at least one box contains two or more objects.

Proof. If every box contained at most one object, there would be at most \( r \) objects altogether. This contradicts the assumption that there are more than \( r \). □

The boxes can be categories rather than physical containers. Among \( 13 \) people, at least two have birthdays in the same month: there are only \( 12 \) possible months.

For a number-theoretic example, take any \( 6 \) integers. Each has one of only five possible remainders modulo \( 5 \), so by the pigeonhole principle at least two of them have the same remainder. Their difference is therefore divisible by \( 5 \).

We will use the following consequence repeatedly: if \( r \) objects occupy \( r \) boxes without repetition, then every box is occupied. If one box were empty, the \( r \) objects would have to fit into at most \( r - 1 \) boxes, forcing a repetition by the principle itself.

5. The Chinese Remainder Theorem

Suppose we know a number’s remainder upon division by two different integers. How much does that tell us about the number? The answer is surprisingly complete, provided the two moduli are coprime.

Chinese Remainder Theorem. Let \( m, n > 1 \) be coprime integers. For any integers \( a \) and \( b \), the system

\[ x \equiv a \pmod{m}, \qquad x \equiv b \pmod{n} \]

has exactly one solution modulo \( mn \).

This means that there is one solution \( x_0 \) among \( 0, 1, \ldots, mn - 1 \), and every integer solution has the form \( x = x_0 + kmn \), where \( k \in \mathbb{Z} \). The symbol \( \mathbb{Z} \) denotes the set of all integers.

A divisibility fact

Before proving the theorem, we need one fact about coprime numbers: if \( m \) and \( n \) are coprime and \( n \mid md \), then \( n \mid d \). The reason is that none of the prime factors of \( n \) occurs in \( m \), so every prime power needed for \( n \) to divide \( md \) must already occur in \( d \). This uses unique prime factorization.

Existence: a pigeonhole proof

Consider these \( n \) numbers:

\[ a, \; a + m, \; a + 2m, \; \ldots, \; a + (n-1)m. \]

All are congruent to \( a \) modulo \( m \). We claim that no two of them have the same remainder modulo \( n \). If \( a + im \equiv a + jm \pmod{n} \) for some \( 0 \le i, j < n \), then \( n \mid (i-j)m \). The divisibility fact above gives \( n \mid i - j \). But \( |i-j| < n \), and the only multiple of \( n \) in that range is \( 0 \). Therefore \( i = j \).

So these \( n \) numbers occupy \( n \) remainder classes modulo \( n \) without repetition. By the consequence of the pigeonhole principle from §4, every class is occupied. In particular, one of the numbers is congruent to \( b \) modulo \( n \), and it satisfies both congruences.

Uniqueness

If \( x \) and \( y \) both satisfy the system, then \( m \) and \( n \) both divide \( x - y \). Since they are coprime, \( mn \) divides \( x - y \). Thus \( x \equiv y \pmod{mn} \). Reducing any solution modulo \( mn \) gives the unique representative between \( 0 \) and \( mn - 1 \). □

Example 2: three remainders

A collection leaves remainder \( 2 \) when grouped in threes, remainder \( 3 \) when grouped in fives, and remainder \( 2 \) when grouped in sevens. The first two conditions give \( x = 8 + 15k \), as in Example 1. The third condition gives

\[ 8 + 15k \equiv 1 + k \equiv 2 \pmod{7}. \]

Hence \( k \equiv 1 \pmod{7} \), so \( k = 1 + 7t \) for an integer \( t \), and

\[ x = 8 + 15(1 + 7t) = 23 + 105t. \]

The smallest positive answer is \( 23 \). Check: \( 23 = 3 \cdot 7 + 2 = 5 \cdot 4 + 3 = 7 \cdot 3 + 2 \).

The theorem extends to any finite collection of pairwise coprime moduli, meaning every pair of them is coprime. Solutions are then unique modulo their product. The two-modulus version is enough to prove the general case: apply it repeatedly, combining the running solution with each new modulus in turn.

Coprimality is essential, not a technical convenience. The conditions \( x \equiv 0 \pmod{2} \) and \( x \equiv 1 \pmod{4} \) have no common solution: the first requires an even number, the second an odd one.
