What Wiles Proved — and What Remained Open

Andrew Wiles proved Fermat’s Last Theorem in 1994. But the theorem he actually proved was not “Fermat’s Last Theorem” — it was a special case of a much larger conjecture about elliptic curves and modular forms. This note explains exactly what Wiles established, what he did not, and how the remaining gap was closed.

1. The conjecture Wiles worked on

In the 1950s, two Japanese mathematicians, Yutaka Taniyama and Goro Shimura, studied the number of solutions of certain equations called elliptic curves. They compared these solution counts to the coefficients of a class of functions called modular forms. The sequences of numbers appeared to match, and Taniyama and Shimura conjectured that this could not be a coincidence: every elliptic curve over the rational numbers should be associated with a modular form. [1]

The conjecture passed through several formulations and gained contributions from André Weil and others. It became known as the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture, or TSW. Its modern name is the modularity theorem.

The statement, in one form, is this:

Modularity theorem (full form). Every elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular.

“Modular” here means that the curve’s sequence of point counts ap matches the coefficients of a modular form of weight 2. The theorem asserts that this matching always occurs.

2. What “semistable” means

The full modularity theorem was far out of reach in the 1980s. Wiles proved a special case: modularity for semistable elliptic curves.

Semistability is a technical condition about how a curve behaves when reduced modulo various primes. Roughly, an elliptic curve is semistable if, at every prime, the reduced curve is either nonsingular or has only a mild singularity called a node — not a cusp. A node is a point where two branches of the curve cross transversally; a cusp is a sharper singularity where the curve comes to a point.

Many elliptic curves are semistable; many are not. The Frey curve constructed from a hypothetical counterexample to Fermat’s Last Theorem is always semistable. This is why Wiles’s special case was enough. He did not need modularity for all elliptic curves — only for the one specific semistable curve that a Fermat counterexample would produce.

3. What Wiles proved in 1995

Wiles published his proof in two papers in 1995.

  • “Modular elliptic curves and Fermat’s Last Theorem” (Annals of Mathematics, 1995). This paper proves the modularity of semistable elliptic curves, and deduces Fermat’s Last Theorem from it via the Frey–Ribet argument.
  • “Ring theoretic properties of certain Hecke algebras” (Annals of Mathematics, 1995), co-authored with Richard Taylor. This paper supplies a crucial technical ingredient — the proof that certain Hecke algebras are local complete intersections — that the first paper relies on.

Together, these two papers establish:

Wiles’s theorem (1995). Every semistable elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular.

This was enough to prove Fermat’s Last Theorem. It was not the full modularity theorem. Elliptic curves that are not semistable were not covered.

4. The gap and how it was fixed

Wiles announced his proof in June 1993, in a series of three lectures at the Isaac Newton Institute in Cambridge. The title of the lectures gave nothing away — “Modular Forms, Elliptic Curves and Galois Representations” — and Wiles wrote up Fermat’s Last Theorem as the conclusion only at the end. The room erupted in applause. [2]

During peer review, however, a serious gap was found. The error was in a step concerning the construction of a “Euler system” — a technical device Wiles had used to bound the size of certain groups. The gap was identified by Nick Katz and reported to Wiles in August 1993. [3]

Wiles spent nearly a year trying to repair the Euler system construction, without success. He then returned to an earlier approach he had abandoned. The new approach required proving that certain Hecke algebras are local complete intersections — a condition that implies the isomorphism he needed. Working with his former student Richard Taylor, Wiles completed this proof in 1994. [3]

The revised proof was submitted on 24 October 1994 and published in May 1995 as a special issue of the Annals of Mathematics.

5. The full modularity theorem (2001)

Wiles’s theorem covered semistable elliptic curves. The remaining cases — curves that are not semistable — were proved later.

  • 1996: Fred Diamond extended Wiles’s techniques to a broader class of curves.
  • 1999: Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond, and Richard Taylor handled further cases.
  • 2001: Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond, and Richard Taylor published the final paper, “On the modularity of elliptic curves over ℚ: wild 3-adic exercises,” completing the proof. [4]

With this work, the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture became the modularity theorem: every elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular, with no exceptions.

So the final picture is:

From Wiles to the full modularity theorem
Year Result Authors
1995 Semistable elliptic curves are modular (enough for FLT) Wiles; Taylor–Wiles
1996–1999 Further cases of modularity Diamond; Conrad–Diamond–Taylor
2001 All elliptic curves over ℚ are modular Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor

6. Summary

  • What Wiles proved: Every semistable elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular. This was enough to imply Fermat’s Last Theorem.
  • What he did not prove: The full modularity theorem for all elliptic curves. Non-semistable curves were not covered by his 1995 papers.
  • What closed the gap: Work by Diamond, Conrad, Taylor, and Breuil between 1996 and 2001 extended the methods to all elliptic curves. The full modularity theorem was proved in 2001.

The distinction matters because it shows how a famous theorem can be proved through a special case of a much larger conjecture. Wiles did not set out to prove the modularity theorem in full. He set out to prove enough of it to settle Fermat’s Last Theorem, and he succeeded.
