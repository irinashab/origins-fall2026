1. Prime or composite

Determine whether each number is prime or composite. Justify each answer:

(a) 187;

(b) 191. For a primality claim, explain why your checks are enough.

Hint for problem 1

For 187, try small prime divisors. For 191, ask how large the smaller factor of a composite number can be: if both factors exceeded √191, what would their product exceed?

Worked answer for problem 1

(a) 187 = 11 · 17, so it is composite.

(b) If 191 were composite, it would have a prime divisor at most √191 < 14. The only primes to check are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. It is odd, its digit sum is 11, and its last digit is not 0 or 5. Also 191 = 7 · 27 + 2 = 11 · 17 + 4 = 13 · 14 + 9. None of those primes divides 191, so 191 is prime. The factor bound follows because a factorization 191 = ab with 1 < a ≤ b implies a² ≤ 191; any prime divisor of a is no larger than a.

2. A finite list of primes

Start with the list 2, 3, 5, 7. Let N = 2 · 3 · 5 · 7 + 1.

(a) Explain why none of the listed primes divides N.

(b) Explain why N must have a prime divisor absent from the list, whether or not N itself is prime.

(c) Why does the same reasoning work for any finite list of primes?

Hint for problem 2

For each listed prime p, compare N with the product N − 1. You may use that every integer greater than 1 has a prime divisor. Do not assume that the new number is prime.

Worked answer for problem 2

Here N = 211. Each listed prime divides 210, so N leaves remainder 1 when divided by each of them. Since N > 1, it has a prime divisor q. That divisor cannot be on the list: if q divided both N and N − 1, it would divide their difference 1, which no prime does. The same argument works with N = p₁···pk + 1 for any nonempty finite list of primes. Thus every finite list misses a prime, proving there are infinitely many. There is no need to determine whether the constructed N is prime.

3. Remainders without large calculations

(a) Find the remainder when −65 is divided by 9, and write a division equation with a nonnegative remainder.

(b) Find the remainder when 2027² − 2027 is divided by 7 without expanding the square.

Hint for problem 3

For (a), find the multiple of 9 immediately below −65. For (b), reduce 2027 first, then apply the same arithmetic operations to its remainder.

Worked answer for problem 3

(a) −65 = 9(−8) + 7, so the remainder is 7. Writing −65 = 9(−7) − 2 is an integer equality but does not use the standard nonnegative remainder.

(b) Since 2027 = 7 · 289 + 4, we have 2027 ≡ 4 (mod 7). Therefore 2027² − 2027 ≡ 4² − 4 = 12 ≡ 5 (mod 7). The remainder is 5.

4. When cancellation fails

A student claims that \(3a \equiv 3b \pmod{12}\) always implies \(a \equiv b \pmod{12}\).

(a) Give a counterexample and check it.

(b) What congruence modulo 4 does follow? Explain directly using divisibility.

Hint for problem 4

Try choosing a and b whose difference is 4. For (b), write 3(a − b) = 12t for an integer t and simplify this integer equality.

Worked answer for problem 4

(a) Take a = 1 and b = 5. Then 3a = 3 and 3b = 15 differ by 12 and are congruent modulo 12. But a and b differ by 4, which is not divisible by 12.

(b) The hypothesis means 3(a − b) = 12t for some integer t. Dividing this equality by 3 gives a − b = 4t, so \(a \equiv b \pmod{4}\). Dividing the equality changes the relevant modulus; it does not justify keeping modulus 12.

5. Using Fermat’s Little Theorem

(a) Use Fermat’s Little Theorem to find the remainder when \(5^{123}\) is divided by 11. Check the hypotheses.

(b) Find the remainder when \(11^{10}\) is divided by 11. Why would applying \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\) here be invalid?

Hint for problem 5

For (a), the relevant exponent is p − 1 = 10. Divide 123 by 10. For (b), check the hypothesis about whether the prime divides the base.

Worked answer for problem 5

(a) The number 11 is prime and does not divide 5, so 510 ≡ 1 (mod 11). Since 123 = 10 · 12 + 3,

\(5^{123}\) = (510)125³ ≡ 5³ = 125 ≡ 4 (mod 11).

The remainder is 4.

(b) The remainder of \(11^{10}\) modulo 11 is 0, since the power is divisible by 11. The nondivisibility hypothesis in the form \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\) fails: the base is itself divisible by the prime.

6. A test that does not prove primality

You are given 91 = 7 · 13 and 3⁶ = 729 ≡ 1 (mod 91).

(a) Deduce that \(3^{90}\) ≡ 1 (mod 91).

(b) Explain why this does not make 91 prime. What does this example show about the converse of Fermat’s Little Theorem?

Hint for problem 6

Express 90 as a multiple of 6. Then distinguish the implication “prime implies this congruence” from its converse.

Worked answer for problem 6

(a) Because 90 = 6 · 15, we obtain \(3^{90}\) = (3⁶)15 ≡ 115 = 1 (mod 91).

(b) Nevertheless 91 = 7 · 13 is composite. Fermat’s Little Theorem says that for a prime p and a base not divisible by p, the stated congruence holds. It does not say that satisfying one such congruence forces the modulus to be prime. This example disproves that converse.

7. Combining three remainders

(a) Find all integers x satisfying \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\) and \(x \equiv 4 \pmod{7}\). State the smallest positive solution.

(b) Add the condition \(x \equiv 0 \pmod{5}\). Find all integer solutions to the three conditions and verify the smallest positive one. Explain the period.

Hint for problem 7

Begin with 1, 5, 9, … and look at their remainders modulo 7. Once you have x = x₀ + 28t, impose the condition modulo 5.

Worked answer for problem 7

(a) Among 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, 25, the number 25 leaves remainder 4 modulo 7. Since 4 and 7 are coprime, all solutions are \(x=25+28t,\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}\). The smallest positive solution is 25.

(b) Modulo 5, the additional condition gives 25 + 28t ≡ 3t ≡ 0. Checking t modulo 5 gives t ≡ 0, so t = 5s. Therefore all integer solutions are \(x=25+140s,\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}\). The smallest positive solution is 25: it leaves remainder 1 modulo 4, remainder 4 modulo 7, and remainder 0 modulo 5. The moduli 4, 7, and 5 are pairwise coprime, so the period is their product 140. For positive solutions only, take s ≥ 0.

8. When the moduli are not coprime

Decide whether each system has a solution. If it does, give all integer solutions; if it does not, explain the contradiction.

(a) \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), \(x \equiv 3 \pmod{6}\).

(b) \(x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\), \(x \equiv 2 \pmod{6}\). Does failure of coprimality automatically mean that no solution exists?

Hint for problem 8

For (a), list the numbers congruent to 1 modulo 4 in one block of length 12. For (b), compare the parities forced by the two conditions.

Worked answer for problem 8

(a) In the range 0 through 11, numbers congruent to 1 modulo 4 are 1, 5, 9; only 9 is congruent to 3 modulo 6. Adding 12 preserves both remainders, and every integer reduces to a representative in that range. Thus all solutions are \(x=9+12t,\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}\).

(b) A number congruent to 1 modulo 4 is odd, whereas a number congruent to 2 modulo 6 is even. No solution exists. Noncoprimality alone does not settle existence: these two examples have the same moduli and different outcomes.

9. Remainder classes as boxes

(a) Prove that among any ten integers, two have a difference divisible by 9. Identify the objects and boxes.

(b) Give nine distinct integers for which no pair has a difference divisible by 9.

Hint for problem 9

Use the possible remainders modulo 9 as boxes. For (b), try choosing exactly one representative of each remainder class.

Worked answer for problem 9

(a) Put each of the ten integers into the box labeled by its remainder modulo 9. There are nine boxes: 0, 1, …, 8. By the pigeonhole principle, two integers a and b occupy the same box. Then a ≡ b (mod 9), so 9 divides a − b. This also works if some of the original integers are equal, because zero is divisible by 9.

(b) The set {0, 1, …, 8} works. Its elements have distinct remainders modulo 9; equivalently, every nonzero difference has absolute value less than 9.

10. Choosing the right boxes

Seven distinct numbers are selected from {1, 2, …, 12}.

(a) Prove that two selected numbers have sum 13. Identify your boxes.

(b) Show that six selected numbers need not contain such a pair.

Hint for problem 10

Pair the numbers in the original set so that each pair sums to 13. How many pairs are there? For (b), choose just one number from each pair.

Worked answer for problem 10

(a) Use the six pairs {1, 12}, {2, 11}, {3, 10}, {4, 9}, {5, 8}, {6, 7} as boxes. Seven selected numbers in six boxes force at least two into one box. Since the selected numbers are distinct and each box contains exactly two numbers, both members of a pair were selected. Their sum is 13.

(b) Choose {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. The greatest sum of two distinct members is 6 + 5 = 11, so no pair sums to 13.

11. Quadratic equations over the real numbers

(a) Solve \(x^2+6x=27\) by completing the square. Explain the missing corner using a sketch or description. Find all real roots and identify the positive length.

(b) Solve \(2x^2-20x+42=0\) by writing the roots as m + u and m − u.

(c) Classify the real roots of \(x^2-8x+k=0\) according to k: two distinct roots, one repeated root, or no real roots. Justify your classification.

Hint for problem 11

For (a), split 6x into two equal rectangles. For (b), first divide the equation by 2; use the sum of the roots to find their average, then their product to find u². For (c), isolate a square and ask whether its required value is positive, zero, or negative.

Worked answer for problem 11

(a) Split 6x into two rectangles of dimensions x by 3. Place them along adjacent sides of the x by x square. The missing corner is 3 by 3, with area 9. Adding 9 to both sides gives

x² + 6x + 9 = 36, so (x + 3)² = 36.
Thus x + 3 = ±6, giving x = 3 or x = −9.

Both solve the real equation; only x = 3 represents a positive side length in the area construction.

(b) Dividing by 2 gives x² − 10x + 21 = 0. The roots have sum 10, so their average is m = 5. Write them as 5 + u and 5 − u. The product condition is 25 − u² = 21, so u² = 4. Choosing u = 2 gives the roots 7 and 3. The factorization 2(x − 7)(x − 3) verifies the answer.

(c) Completing the square gives \((x-4)^2=16-k\). If k < 16, the roots are the two distinct real numbers \(4\pm\sqrt{16-k}\). If k = 16, the root 4 is repeated. If k > 16, the right side is negative, so there are no real roots.

12. Fermat’s Last Theorem

(a) State Fermat’s Last Theorem precisely. Explain how it differs from Fermat’s Little Theorem.

(b) Optional extension: suppose positive integers a, b, c satisfied \(a^{15}+b^{15}=c^{15}\). Show how this would produce a counterexample with exponent 3, and also one with exponent 5.

Hint for problem 12

For (a), specify the exponent and the domain of x, y, z. For (b), use 15 = 3 · 5 and the identity \((a^r)^s=a^{rs}\).

Worked answer for problem 12

(a) For every integer n > 2, the equation \(x^n+y^n=z^n\) has no solutions in positive integers x, y, z. The restrictions to integer exponent n > 2 and positive integer variables are essential. Fermat’s Little Theorem instead concerns congruences: if p is prime and p does not divide a, then \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\) (mod p). The two theorems address different questions.

(b) A hypothetical solution with exponent 15 would give

\((a^5)^3+(b^5)^3=(c^5)^3\),

a solution with exponent 3 in positive integers. It would also give

\((a^3)^5+(b^3)^5=(c^3)^5\),

a solution with exponent 5. Integer powers of positive integers are again positive integers, so both constructions stay in the required domain. This illustrates how a solution at a composite exponent can produce one at a divisor exponent.

Checking your explanations

  • A primality argument rules out every possible prime divisor up to the square root; a composite verdict only needs one nontrivial factorization.
  • A proof that primes never run out needs a new prime divisor, not a claim that the constructed number is always prime.
  • A remainder is nonnegative and less than the modulus. Check theorem hypotheses before reducing an exponent or cancelling a factor.
  • A Chinese remainder answer should include all solutions and their period, then verify the stated remainders.
  • A pigeonhole proof names the objects and boxes and explains why sharing a box gives the desired conclusion.
  • A quadratic solution accounts for both square-root signs and distinguishes real algebraic solutions from positive lengths.
