Why prove something that looks obvious?

In our discussion of Chinese mathematics, we encountered procedures, diagrams, and explanations of why methods work. Keep that question with you as we turn to early Greek mathematics: what makes an explanation general?

Consider the sums 1 = 1², 1 + 3 = 2², and 1 + 3 + 5 = 3². The next case is easy to predict. But how could we explain why the pattern continues, however many terms we take?

Today we move from Thales’ circle geometry to Pythagoras and his school, the right-triangle theorem, and the arrangement of whole numbers as dots. In each, a picture suggests a result; our task is to identify the reasoning that makes the picture apply beyond a particular example.

What can we know about the beginnings?

Cummings opens his chapter with a question: “The First Proof in History?” His discussion traces the attribution to Thales through later writers, especially Proclus and the lost history of Eudemus. This is a chain of testimony, not a surviving manuscript by Thales. We will distinguish the validity of an argument from certainty about who first gave it.

Likewise, this lesson does not require us to imagine that earlier mathematical traditions lacked reasoning. We are examining a particular tradition of proof and its later presentation.

Thales and the circle

A diameter bisects a circle

Cummings’s first example is deliberately simple: any diameter divides a circle into two equal halves. A circle consists of points at a fixed distance from its center. A diameter passes through that center.

Imagine folding the circle along the diameter. Why must the two arcs coincide? Reflection across the diameter leaves the center fixed and preserves distance. Every reflected point is therefore still at the same radius from the center. The reflected semicircle coincides with the other semicircle.

Cummings presents this as a contradiction argument: a mismatch would require unequal radial distances where both must equal the circle’s radius. The definition of a circle does the essential work. Folding a paper circle illustrates the argument; the distance-preserving property explains why it works for any circle.

Thales’ theorem

If AB is a diameter and C is any other point on the circle, then angle ACB is a right angle. The point C need not be at the top or in a symmetric position.

We will investigate this through Cummings’s Exercise 3.2, using rectangles. Treat the argument as a possible reconstruction, not as a preserved original proof by Thales.

Guided exercise: discovering Thales’ theorem

Adapted from Cummings, Exercise 3.2. Allow approximately 10–12 minutes.

Explore first

  1. Draw a wide rectangle and a tall rectangle.
  2. In each, draw both diagonals and mark their intersection O.
  3. Draw a circle centered at O through one corner. Explain why it passes through all four corners.
  4. Choose one diagonal. What angle does it subtend at either remaining corner?

Now return to an arbitrary circle with diameter AB and a point C on its circumference. Explain why angle ACB must be 90°.

Hint 1: distances to the corners

A rectangle’s diagonals are equal and bisect each other. Its four half-diagonals therefore have the same length. What does that say about a circle centered at their intersection?

Hint 2: start with any point C

Draw the diameter CD through C and the center O. Consider quadrilateral ACBD. Its diagonals are AB and CD.

Hint 3: recognize the quadrilateral

The diagonals bisect each other, so ACBD is a parallelogram. They are equal as well. A parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle.

Worked answer

In a rectangle, the four half-diagonals have equal lengths. All four vertices consequently lie on a circle centered at their intersection. Each diagonal is a diameter, and the angle it subtends at another corner is a right angle.

To establish the result for every point C, let D be the point opposite C on the circle. The diagonals AB and CD of quadrilateral ACBD bisect each other at O, so ACBD is a parallelogram. Both diagonals are diameters of the same circle, so they are equal. Thus ACBD is a rectangle, and its corner angle ACB is 90°.

Why does the rectangle criterion work? In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal. If the diagonals are also equal, the triangles ABC and BAD are congruent by side–side–side: AB is shared, BC = AD, and AC = BD. Hence the adjacent angles ABC and BAD are equal. Adjacent angles of a parallelogram sum to 180°, so each is 90°. Apply this fact with the vertices ordered A–C–B–D in our circle diagram.

Discuss: What did the initial drawings help us discover? What does the final construction establish that several drawings alone could not?

Pythagoras and the Pythagorean community

Pythagoras came from Samos and lived in the sixth century BCE. He established a community at Croton, a Greek city in southern Italy. Its interests included mathematics, philosophy, religious practices, and a shared way of life. The school continued beyond its founder, so we should distinguish Pythagoras himself from the generations of Pythagoreans who followed him.

Cummings emphasizes how difficult the evidence is. Later accounts mix mathematical achievements with extraordinary legends, and discoveries within a community could be credited to its leader. We have no surviving proof written by Pythagoras. Even whether he personally proved the theorem bearing his name remains uncertain. The name records a tradition of attribution; it does not settle the question of priority.

Whole numbers and the order of the world

The Pythagorean tradition associated number with order and harmony. Studying whole numbers could therefore be more than a computational activity: their relationships might reveal something about the world. Number symbolism belonged to this outlook, but so did investigations whose mathematical content we can still examine.

For example, 6 equals the sum of its positive proper divisors: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. So does 28: 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14 = 28. Such numbers are called perfect. This gives a taste of the study of numbers for their own properties; we will leave their systematic construction for another occasion.

Musical ratios

Lewinter and Widulski use musical intervals to illustrate the attraction of simple ratios. For ideal strings of the same material and thickness under the same tension, the frequency is inversely proportional to the vibrating length. Halving a string’s length doubles its frequency, producing an octave. Length ratios 3:2 and 4:3 correspond to a perfect fifth and a perfect fourth, respectively, with the shorter string sounding higher.

These relationships help explain the appeal of a world understood through number. They do not establish every story told about Pythagoras’s own experiments. Keep the mathematical relationship separate from the biography.

Figurate numbers belong in this setting: a collection of counters can display the structure of a whole number as a triangle or a square. We will return to those arrangements after the theorem for which Pythagoras is most famous.

The famous theorem: squares on the sides

For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, a² + b² = c². Geometrically, the two squares constructed on the legs have the same total area as the square constructed on the hypotenuse. It is a statement about every right triangle, not only triangles with whole-number sides.

The example 3² + 4² = 5² verifies one instance. Knowing a triple, stating a general relationship, and proving that relationship are different achievements. Cummings’s discussion of the theorem around the world makes these distinctions particularly useful.

Who else gave proofs?

  • Zhao Shuang, China, third century CE: his commentary on the Zhoubi suanjing includes the famous diagram of a square and four right triangles. Cummings discusses how its area relationships can be interpreted and generalized as a dissection proof (p. 137). This connects with the gougu geometry of our previous lecture.
  • Bhāskara II, India, twelfth century: a dissection proof arranges four copies of a right triangle inside a square whose side is the hypotenuse. We work through this argument below.
  • James A. Garfield, United States, 1876: while serving in Congress, before becoming president, he published a proof that computes a trapezoid’s area in two ways. The pieces are two copies of the original triangle and a third right triangle.
  • Euclid, around 300 BCE: his proof appears in Elements, Book I, Proposition 47. We will study its construction and place in his deductive system later.

The historical comparisons of Bhāskara, Garfield, and Euclid are also presented in the University of Cambridge’s Proving Pythagoras: historical background. For Garfield’s 1876 publication, see MathWorld’s account.

There is no need to choose just one of these arguments as the proof. Different proofs expose different relationships: rearrangement, equality of areas, or similarity. Nor does a later proof tell us what Pythagoras’s own argument was.

A dissection proof associated with Bhāskara II

Take four congruent copies of a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c. Name the longer leg b, so b > a for the diagram. Arrange the triangles as shown. Their four hypotenuses form the outside boundary; their legs surround a small central square.

  1. Why is the outer boundary a square? All four sides are hypotenuses of congruent triangles, so each has length c. At each outer vertex, the two acute angles of the original right triangle meet. They sum to 90°, so all four outer angles are right angles.
  2. What is left in the middle? The exposed segments of the legs have length b − a. They form a square, since the legs meet at right angles. Its area is (b − a)².
  3. Count the same area in two ways. The outer square has area c². The four triangles have total area 4(ab/2) = 2ab. Add the central square:

c² = 4(ab/2) + (b − a)²

= 2ab + b² − 2ab + a²

= a² + b².

If a = b, the central gap disappears: the four triangles meet at one point and the same area equation holds with (b − a)² = 0. Thus the equal-leg case is included as well.

Pause at the picture

Which step would be missing if we simply announced that the outside is a square because it looks like one? Explain how the angle argument supplies that step. Notice that we used triangle angles and areas; we did not assume the theorem we were proving.

With legs 3 and 4, the four triangle areas total 24 and the central square has area 1. The outer square therefore has area 25. This illustrates the construction; the argument with a and b establishes the general result.

Numbers made visible

Returning to the Pythagorean interest in whole numbers, Burton’s discussion of figurate numbers gives us another way to connect a picture with a general argument. Arrange counters in triangular rows of 1, 2, 3, and so on. The first triangular numbers are 1, 3, 6, and 10. Let Tn denote the total in n rows.

Two triangles make a rectangle

Take two copies of the same triangular array. Rotate one and fit the two together to form a rectangle with n rows and n + 1 columns. Each copy contains Tn dots, so:

2Tn = n(n + 1),   therefore   Tn = n(n + 1)/2.

We have explained the sum 1 + 2 + ··· + n. The letters record the arrangement in modern notation; the construction supplies its meaning.

Odd numbers build squares

Start with one dot. Add an L-shaped border of three dots to make a 2 × 2 square. Add five more to make a 3 × 3 square; add seven to make a 4 × 4 square.

To grow an (n − 1) × (n − 1) square into an n × n square, add one row of n dots and one column of n − 1 additional dots. The border contains 2n − 1 dots. Thus:

1 + 3 + 5 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n².

The opening pattern now has an explanation. Every stage adds exactly the next odd number and produces exactly the next square.

Two consecutive triangles make a square

A square array of n² dots can be split along its diagonal into a triangular array with n rows and another with n − 1 rows. Consequently:

Tn + Tn−1 = n².

For example, 10 + 6 = 16. Sketch the two parts inside a 4 × 4 array, then explain how your division would work in a larger square.

Algebra confirms the same relationship:

n(n + 1)/2 + (n − 1)n/2 = 2n²/2 = n².

Closing and afterthoughts

In the circle argument, equal distances and the properties of rectangles forced a right angle. In Bhāskara’s dissection, the areas of four triangles and a small square established the right-triangle theorem. In the dot arrays, rearrangement forced an identity. A useful picture makes a relationship visible; an explanation tells us why its structure persists in every permitted case.
