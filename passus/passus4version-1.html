A journey across the Mediterranean

Our last journey took us to China, where counting rods could be moved across a board and geometric figures could be cut apart to explain a calculation. We now turn west, to Greek-speaking communities around the Mediterranean. The setting changes, but a familiar question travels with us: how can we know that a mathematical method will always work?

Begin in Miletus, on the Aegean coast of what is now Turkey. In the sixth century BCE, this was a Greek city connected by sea to other trading communities. Greek mathematics developed in a world of contact with Egypt and western Asia. Measuring land, navigating, building, and keeping accounts already had long histories. Mathematical knowledge did not begin on an empty shore.

Thales of Miletus belongs to this world. Later generations remembered him as a philosopher and a geometer, a person who sought explanations for things others might simply accept. His mathematical writings have not survived. Much of the story reaches us through later authors, including Proclus, who drew on earlier accounts such as the now-lost history of Eudemus. That distance matters: we can examine the arguments, but we cannot place a surviving notebook of Thales beside them.

Imagine being asked to explain why a diameter divides a circle equally. You might reply, “Just look at it.” Yet a roughly drawn circle is never exact, and looking at one circle tells us little about every possible circle. The interesting step is to ask what a circle is, and what follows from that definition.

Five theorems associated with Thales

Five geometric results are traditionally associated with Thales. They are small enough to state in ordinary language, yet together they suggest a new ambition: to explain properties of whole classes of figures.

  1. A diameter bisects a circle. Every diameter divides the circle and its enclosed region into two congruent halves.
  2. The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal. If AB = AC, then the angles at B and C are equal.
  3. Vertically opposite angles are equal. When two straight lines cross, each angle equals the angle directly opposite it.
  4. A side and its two adjacent angles determine a triangle up to congruence. If two triangles have an equal corresponding side and equal angles at both ends of that side, they have the same size and shape. We call this the angle–side–angle criterion.
  5. An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle. If AB is a diameter and C is any other point on the circle, then angle ACB = 90°.

“Associated with” is deliberate. These are traditional attributions, not five proofs preserved in Thales’ handwriting. Their mathematical value, however, is something we can judge for ourselves. Let us start with the first.

Why a diameter divides a circle equally

The first diagram above shows the impossible mismatch we will rule out: after folding, two boundaries cannot lie at different distances from the same center.

Let O be the center, let r be the radius, and draw a diameter AB. Every point on the circle is exactly distance r from O. Now imagine folding the figure along AB, bringing one side onto the other. In geometric language, we reflect the figure across the line AB.

The center O lies on the fold, so it stays fixed. Folding preserves lengths. Therefore a point P on the arc, originally at distance r from O, lands at a point P′ that is also at distance r from O. By the definition of a circle, P′ belongs to the same circle.

Could the two arcs nevertheless fail to match? Suppose one stuck out beyond the other. Along a ray from O through that mismatch, the two boundaries would lie at different distances from O. But both distances must be r. That is impossible. Every point on one arc has its reflected partner on the other; reflecting back reverses the correspondence.

The same reflection matches the enclosed regions as well: points inside the circle remain at distances less than r from O. Thus the two halves coincide exactly. They are congruent, and consequently have equal areas.

A folded paper circle lets us see the idea. The proof goes further: equal distances from the center explain why the halves must match, regardless of the circle’s size or the direction of the diameter.

A right angle that follows a moving point

The fifth theorem is more surprising. Fix a diameter AB, choose a point C anywhere else on the circle, and join C to A and B. Move C around the upper arc. The triangle becomes tall, flat, or lopsided, but its angle at C remains a right angle. Nothing in the statement requires C to sit directly above the center.

To discover why, we will begin with a figure whose right angles are already familiar: a rectangle.

Guided exercise: discovering Thales’ theorem

Draw the figures before opening the hints. The aim is to turn an observation about rectangles into an explanation for every point on a circle.

Explore first

  1. Draw a wide rectangle and a tall rectangle.
  2. In each, draw both diagonals and mark their intersection O.
  3. Draw a circle centered at O through one corner. Explain why it passes through all four corners.
  4. Choose one diagonal. What angle does it subtend at either remaining corner?

Now return to an arbitrary circle with diameter AB and a point C on its circumference. Explain why angle ACB must be 90°.

Hint 1: distances to the corners

A rectangle’s diagonals are equal and bisect each other. Its four half-diagonals therefore have the same length. What does that say about a circle centered at their intersection?

Hint 2: start with any point C

Draw the diameter CD through C and the center O. Consider quadrilateral ACBD. Its diagonals are AB and CD.

Hint 3: recognize the quadrilateral

The diagonals bisect each other, so ACBD is a parallelogram. They are equal as well. A parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle.

Worked answer

In a rectangle, the four half-diagonals have equal lengths. All four vertices consequently lie on a circle centered at their intersection. Each diagonal is a diameter, and the angle it subtends at another corner is a right angle.

To establish the result for every point C, let D be the point opposite C on the circle. The diagonals AB and CD of quadrilateral ACBD bisect each other at O, so ACBD is a parallelogram. Both diagonals are diameters of the same circle, so they are equal. Thus ACBD is a rectangle, and its corner angle ACB is 90°.

Why does the rectangle criterion work? In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal. If the diagonals are also equal, the triangles ABC and BAD are congruent by side–side–side: AB is shared, BC = AD, and AC = BD. Hence the adjacent angles ABC and BAD are equal. Adjacent angles of a parallelogram sum to 180°, so each is 90°. Apply this fact with the vertices ordered A–C–B–D in our circle diagram.

Discuss: What did the initial drawings help us discover? What does the final construction establish that several drawings alone could not?

Pythagoras and the Pythagorean community

Pythagoras came from Samos and lived in the sixth century BCE. He established a community at Croton, a Greek city in southern Italy. Its interests included mathematics, philosophy, religious practices, and a shared way of life. The school continued beyond its founder, so we should distinguish Pythagoras himself from the generations of Pythagoreans who followed him.

Later accounts mix mathematical achievements with extraordinary legends, and discoveries within a community could be credited to its leader. We have no surviving proof written by Pythagoras. Even whether he personally proved the theorem bearing his name remains uncertain. The name records a tradition of attribution; it does not settle the question of priority.

Whole numbers and the order of the world

The Pythagorean tradition associated number with order and harmony. Studying whole numbers could therefore be more than a computational activity: their relationships might reveal something about the world. Number symbolism belonged to this outlook, but so did investigations whose mathematical content we can still examine.

For example, 6 equals the sum of its positive proper divisors: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. So does 28: 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14 = 28. Such numbers are called perfect. Compare 12: its proper divisors, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6, total 16, so it is called abundant. The proper divisors of 10 total only 8, so 10 is deficient. A familiar act—finding divisors—has become a way of classifying numbers by their internal structure.

Finding perfect numbers

Finding 6 and 28 invites a question: can we produce more perfect numbers without testing every integer? Start with 1 and keep doubling. Add the numbers as you go:

1 + 2 = 3
1 + 2 + 4 = 7
1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15
1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 = 31.

Each total is one less than the next power of two. To see why, call the last term L and the sum S. Write the sum, then double it:

S = 1 + 2 + 4 + ··· + L
2S = 2 + 4 + 8 + ··· + 2L.

Subtract the first equation from the second. All the shared terms cancel; only the final 2L and the initial −1 remain. Therefore S = 2L − 1.

Now comes the connection with perfect numbers. If S is prime, multiply it by L. The product LS is a perfect number. The prime condition is essential: we keep the totals 3, 7, 31, and 127, but skip 15 and 63.

3 × 2 = 6
7 × 4 = 28
31 × 16 = 496
127 × 64 = 8128.

Why should this work? Consider 28 = 4 × 7. Its proper divisors fall into two groups: 1, 2, 4 and 7, 14. The first group totals 7; the second totals 7(1 + 2) = 21. Together they give 28.

The same reasoning works in general. Since L is a power of two and S is an odd prime, every divisor of LS is either a power of two or S times a power of two. The proper divisors are exactly:

1, 2, 4, …, L   and   S, 2S, 4S, …, (L/2)S.

The first group totals S. The second totals S(L − 1), because 1 + 2 + ··· + L/2 = L − 1. Thus the sum of the proper divisors is

S + S(L − 1) = LS,

which is the number itself. The pattern has become a construction with a reason behind it. This method is recorded in Euclid’s Elements; here we have expressed its arithmetic in modern notation.

Hearing a ratio

Number also seemed to reach beyond counting. A plucked string produces a sound, and the sound changes when the vibrating part of the string becomes shorter. A guitar player does this by pressing a string against a fret. A simple instrument for studying the relationship is a monochord: one string stretched over a sounding box, with a movable bridge that changes the length allowed to vibrate.

Imagine a string whose vibrating length is 60 centimeters. Keep its tension and thickness unchanged. Pluck the whole length, then move the bridge so that only 30 centimeters vibrate. The shorter string sounds an octave higher. If the full string vibrates 240 times each second, the half-length vibrates 480 times each second. These modern frequency numbers help us describe the experiment; the ancient comparison could be made by ear and by measuring lengths.

The two lengths stand in the ratio 2:1. Their frequencies also stand in a 2:1 ratio, but in the opposite order: the shorter string has the higher frequency. Under these fixed conditions, frequency is inversely proportional to vibrating length.

For the fifth, shorten the string to two-thirds of its original length: 40 centimeters. The frequency becomes three-halves of the original, or 360 Hz. For the fourth, use three-quarters of the length: 45 centimeters. The frequency becomes four-thirds of the original, or 320 Hz. If the starting note is called C, these intervals take us to F, G, and the next C in a tuning based on these simple ratios.

The names count positions in a scale: C–D–E–F spans a fourth; C–D–E–F–G spans a fifth. They do not mean multiplying the frequency by four or five. An octave reaches the eighth named position, where the letter name repeats.

There is arithmetic in combining intervals, too. Go up a perfect fifth and then a perfect fourth. The frequency ratios multiply:

(3/2) × (4/3) = 2.

The result is an octave. A relationship that can be heard can also be expressed with the small whole numbers 2, 3, and 4. This helps us understand why the Pythagorean tradition connected harmony with numerical order. It does not mean that every musical preference reduces to a ratio, or that all later tuning systems use precisely these intervals.

Stories about Pythagoras personally discovering these relationships were told much later. The reliable classroom starting point is the string experiment itself. Its conditions matter: changing tension or using a thicker string also changes the pitch. We compare lengths while holding those other features fixed.

From this point of view, arranging counters into triangles or investigating the sides of a right triangle belonged to a larger search for order. Let us turn first to the triangle that has carried Pythagoras’s name for centuries.

The famous theorem: squares on the sides

For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, a² + b² = c². Geometrically, the two squares constructed on the legs have the same total area as the square constructed on the hypotenuse. It is a statement about every right triangle, not only triangles with whole-number sides.

A triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5 gives us 9 + 16 = 25. But the theorem also concerns right triangles with sides that are not whole numbers. A list of numerical examples cannot exhaust them. To understand its history, we must distinguish recognizing useful triples, stating the general rule, and explaining why the rule always holds.

A theorem with many lives

The relationship was known in forms that predate Pythagoras. Babylonian mathematical records contain right-triangle triples, and the Indian Śulbasūtras state a general relation between a rectangle’s diagonal and its sides. Such evidence tells us that the mathematics had a wider history than its familiar Greek name suggests. It does not provide us with a surviving proof by Pythagoras.

In China, the right-triangle relation was called gougu, after the two legs. Zhao Shuang, writing in the third century CE, accompanied his commentary on the Zhoubi suanjing with a diagram involving a square and four right triangles. Its pieces make an area argument visible. We met this family of ideas in the previous lecture: move the pieces, preserve their areas, and compare what remains.

Centuries later, the Indian mathematician Bhāskara II, who lived in the twelfth century, gave a compact dissection proof. Four copies of a right triangle fit around a small square, and their hypotenuses become the sides of a larger square. We will follow that construction in a moment.

The search for new proofs continued long after the theorem was secure. In 1876, James A. Garfield, then a member of the United States Congress and later president, published a proof using a trapezoid. He calculated its area once from its parallel sides and height, and again by adding the areas of three triangles. The equality of those two calculations yields the same theorem.

Euclid also supplied a proof in his Elements, around 300 BCE. His construction belongs to a carefully organized sequence of propositions; we will encounter it when we reach his work. Here, the route through cut-and-rearranged figures lets us see another kind of mathematical explanation.

Why prove a result again? A new argument can reveal something the old one concealed. One proof emphasizes similarity, another rearrangement, another the measurement of areas. The conclusion remains the same, but the connections we see become richer.

A dissection proof associated with Bhāskara II

Take four congruent copies of a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c. Name the longer leg b, so b > a for the diagram. Arrange the triangles as shown. Their four hypotenuses form the outside boundary; their legs surround a small central square.

  1. Why is the outer boundary a square? All four sides are hypotenuses of congruent triangles, so each has length c. At each outer vertex, the two acute angles of the original right triangle meet. They sum to 90°, so all four outer angles are right angles.
  2. What is left in the middle? The exposed segments of the legs have length b − a. They form a square, since the legs meet at right angles. Its area is (b − a)².
  3. Count the same area in two ways. The outer square has area c². The four triangles have total area 4(ab/2) = 2ab. Add the central square:

c² = 4(ab/2) + (b − a)²

= 2ab + b² − 2ab + a²

= a² + b².

If a = b, the central gap disappears: the four triangles meet at one point and the same area equation holds with (b − a)² = 0. Thus the equal-leg case is included as well.

Pause at the picture

Which step would be missing if we simply announced that the outside is a square because it looks like one? Explain how the angle argument supplies that step. Notice that we used triangle angles and areas; we did not assume the theorem we were proving.

With legs 3 and 4, the four triangle areas total 24 and the central square has area 1. The outer square therefore has area 25. This illustrates the construction; the argument with a and b establishes the general result.

Numbers made visible

Now return to a handful of counters. To study a whole number, we can count the collection, but we can also arrange it. A Pythagorean interest in the properties of numbers finds a natural expression in these shapes. Place one counter in the first row, two beneath it, then three, and so on. The first triangular numbers are 1, 3, 6, and 10. Let Tn denote the total in n rows.

Two triangles make a rectangle

Take two copies of the same triangular array. Rotate one and fit the two together to form a rectangle with n rows and n + 1 columns. Each copy contains Tn dots, so:

2Tn = n(n + 1),   therefore   Tn = n(n + 1)/2.

We have explained the sum 1 + 2 + ··· + n. The letters record the arrangement in modern notation; the construction supplies its meaning.

Odd numbers build squares

Start with one dot. Add an L-shaped border of three dots to make a 2 × 2 square. Add five more to make a 3 × 3 square; add seven to make a 4 × 4 square.

To grow an (n − 1) × (n − 1) square into an n × n square, add one row of n dots and one column of n − 1 additional dots. The border contains 2n − 1 dots. Thus:

1 + 3 + 5 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n².

The opening pattern now has an explanation. Every stage adds exactly the next odd number and produces exactly the next square.

Two consecutive triangles make a square

A square array of n² dots can be split along its diagonal into a triangular array with n rows and another with n − 1 rows. Consequently:

Tn + Tn−1 = n².

For example, 10 + 6 = 16. Sketch the two parts inside a 4 × 4 array, then explain how your division would work in a larger square.

Algebra confirms the same relationship:

n(n + 1)/2 + (n − 1)n/2 = 2n²/2 = n².

Afterthoughts: What a picture can explain

In the circle argument, equal distances and the properties of rectangles forced a right angle. In Bhāskara’s dissection, the areas of four triangles and a small square established the right-triangle theorem. In the dot arrays, rearrangement forced an identity. A useful picture makes a relationship visible; an explanation tells us why its structure persists in every permitted case.
