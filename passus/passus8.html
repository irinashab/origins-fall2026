Euclid proved that there are infinitely many prime numbers. But infinity does not tell us how frequently primes occur. Are they distributed according to a pattern? Can their properties help us solve equations in whole numbers? These questions lead from elementary counting and remainders to some of the deepest ideas in mathematics.

1. Counting primes: the function π(N)

We already know what prime and composite numbers are. Our new question is how to count the primes up to a given bound. Define the prime-counting function

π(N) = the number of primes less than or equal to N.

Here π names a function; it does not mean the circle constant. For example, π(10) = 4 because the primes at most 10 are 2, 3, 5, and 7. Notice that

π(10) = 4,   π(11) = 5,   π(12) = 5.

The function increases by 1 whenever we reach a prime and stays unchanged at other integers. Its graph is a staircase with unevenly spaced steps.

2. The Prime Number Theorem

Although individual primes are difficult to predict, their overall number follows a regular law.

Prime Number Theorem. As N grows without bound,

π(N) ∼ N / log N.

Here log N is the natural logarithm, the logarithm to base e, where e ≈ 2.71828. Equivalently, log N is the number t for which et = N. The symbol ∼, read “is asymptotic to,” means that the ratio approaches 1:

π(N) / (N / log N) → 1 as N → ∞.

The theorem concerns relative error. It does not say that the difference between the count and the estimate approaches zero. In the table, the absolute difference grows even while the relative discrepancy decreases.

Dividing by N gives π(N)/N ∼ 1/log N. The left side is the proportion of integers from 1 to N that are prime. Since log N grows without bound, that proportion tends to zero. There are infinitely many primes, yet they occupy a decreasing proportion of the integers.

Jacques Hadamard and Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin independently proved the theorem in 1896, using ideas connected with Riemann’s zeta function. We will return to that function at the end of the lecture. [1]

3. Divisibility, remainders, and congruence

Recall that d ∣ a means that a = dk for some integer k. For a positive integer m, we write

a ≡ b (mod m)

when m divides a − b. We say that a and b are congruent modulo m: they have the same remainder upon division by m. For example, 17 ≡ 2 (mod 5), because 17 − 2 = 15 is divisible by 5.

The number m is the modulus. Every integer belongs to one of the m remainder classes 0, 1, …, m − 1. Congruences can be added and multiplied. Thus 17 ≡ 2 (mod 5) implies 17² ≡ 2² ≡ 4 (mod 5). Large powers can often be studied through small remainders.

Two positive integers are coprime if their greatest common divisor is 1. For example, 8 and 15 are coprime even though neither is prime.

4. The pigeonhole principle

Pigeonhole principle. If more than r objects are placed into r boxes, where r is a positive integer, at least one box contains two or more objects.

Proof. If every box contained at most one object, there would be at most r objects altogether. This contradicts the assumption that there are more than r. □

The boxes can be categories. Among 13 people, at least two have birthdays in the same month: there are only 12 possible months. Among any 6 integers, at least two have the same remainder modulo 5: there are only five remainder classes. Their difference is therefore divisible by 5.

We will use the following consequence: if r objects occupy r boxes without repetition, every box is occupied. If one box were empty, r objects would have to fit into at most r − 1 boxes, forcing a repetition.

5. The Chinese Remainder Theorem

Suppose we know a number’s remainder upon division by two different integers. How much does that tell us about the number?

Chinese Remainder Theorem. Let m, n > 1 be coprime integers. For any integers a and b, the system

x ≡ a (mod m),   x ≡ b (mod n)

has exactly one solution modulo mn.

This means that there is one solution x₀ among 0, 1, …, mn − 1, and every integer solution has the form x = x₀ + kmn, where k ∈ ℤ. The symbol ℤ denotes the set of all integers.

A divisibility fact

If m and n are coprime and n ∣ md, then n ∣ d. Indeed, none of the prime factors of n occurs in m. Therefore every prime power needed for n to divide md must occur in d. This uses unique prime factorization.

Existence: a pigeonhole proof

Consider these n numbers:

a, a + m, a + 2m, …, a + (n − 1)m.

All are congruent to a modulo m. We claim that no two have the same remainder modulo n. If a + im ≡ a + jm (mod n), then n ∣ (i − j)m. Coprimality gives n ∣ i − j. But 0 ≤ i, j < n, so |i − j| < n. The only multiple of n in this range is 0; therefore i = j.

These n numbers occupy n remainder classes without repetition. By the pigeonhole principle, every class occurs. One of the numbers must therefore be congruent to b modulo n, and it satisfies both conditions.

Uniqueness

If x and y satisfy both congruences, then m and n both divide x − y. Since they are coprime, mn divides x − y. Thus x ≡ y (mod mn). Reducing any solution modulo mn gives the unique representative between 0 and mn − 1. □

Example 1: two remainders

Solve x ≡ 2 (mod 3) and x ≡ 3 (mod 5). List five numbers satisfying the first condition:

The number 8 satisfies both conditions. All solutions are x = 8 + 15k, k ∈ ℤ.

Example 2: three remainders

A collection leaves remainder 2 when grouped in threes, remainder 3 when grouped in fives, and remainder 2 when grouped in sevens. The first two conditions give x = 8 + 15k. The third gives

8 + 15k ≡ 1 + k ≡ 2 (mod 7).

Hence k = 1 + 7t for an integer t, so

x = 8 + 15(1 + 7t) = 23 + 105t.

The smallest positive answer is 23. Check: 23 = 3·7 + 2 = 5·4 + 3 = 7·3 + 2.

The theorem extends to any finite collection of pairwise coprime moduli, meaning every pair is coprime. Solutions are unique modulo their product; the two-modulus theorem can be applied repeatedly.

Coprimality matters. The conditions x ≡ 0 (mod 2) and x ≡ 1 (mod 4) have no common solution: one requires an even number and the other an odd number.

6. Pythagorean triples: from examples to a formula

A Pythagorean triple is a triple of positive integers (a, b, c) satisfying a² + b² = c². These are the side lengths of a right triangle with integer sides, with c the hypotenuse.

Multiplying every entry by the same positive integer gives another triple: (3, 4, 5) becomes (6, 8, 10). A triple is primitive if its entries have no common divisor greater than 1. Dividing by the greatest common divisor shows that every triple is a positive integer multiple of a primitive one.

Properties of primitive triples

The entries are pairwise coprime. If a prime divided two entries, the equation would force it to divide the square of the third, and hence the third itself. This contradicts primitivity.

Exactly one leg is even. Both cannot be even in a primitive triple. Both cannot be odd either: an odd square is 1 modulo 4, so their sum would be 2 modulo 4. But squares are only 0 or 1 modulo 4. Thus one leg is even and the other odd; the hypotenuse is odd.

Deriving the formula

Let a be the odd leg and b the even leg. Factor the difference of squares:

b² = c² − a² = (c − a)(c + a),

(b/2)² = [(c + a)/2] · [(c − a)/2].

The factors on the right are positive integers. They are coprime, because a common divisor would divide both their sum c and their difference a.

A product of coprime positive integers is a square only if each factor is a square. By unique prime factorization, each prime appears in only one factor, and its exponent must be even. Therefore, for integers m > n > 0,

(c + a)/2 = m²,   (c − a)/2 = n².

Adding and subtracting yields Euclid’s formula:

a = m² − n²,   b = 2mn,   c = m² + n².

The numbers m and n are coprime, since their squares are coprime. They have opposite parity—one even and one odd—because c is odd. Conversely, coprime m > n > 0 of opposite parity produce a primitive triple.

To verify the converse, expand (m² − n²)² + (2mn)² = (m² + n²)². The first leg is odd. Any odd prime dividing both legs would divide 2mn, hence m or n, and then m² − n² would force it to divide the other. That contradicts coprimality. Thus no prime divides both legs, and the triple is primitive.

7. Gaussian integers and Gaussian primes

A difference of squares factors as a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). To factor a sum of squares similarly, introduce i with i² = −1. Then

a² + b² = (a + bi)(a − bi).

A Gaussian integer is a complex number a + bi with a, b ∈ ℤ. The set is written ℤ[i]. Geometrically, Gaussian integers are the points with integer coordinates in the complex plane: a is the horizontal coordinate and b the vertical coordinate.

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication stay within this set. For example, (2 + i)(3 − i) = 7 + i.

Conjugates, norms, and units

The conjugate of z = a + bi is z̄ = a − bi. Its norm is

N(z) = zz̄ = a² + b².

The norm is a nonnegative integer and is multiplicative: N(zw) = N(z)N(w). This follows from (zw)̅ = z̄w̄ and rearranging zwz̄w̄. For example, N(2 + i) = 5, N(3 − i) = 10, and N(7 + i) = 50.

A unit is a number whose multiplicative inverse also belongs to the same number system. The Gaussian units are 1, −1, i, and −i. Indeed, zw = 1 implies N(z)N(w) = 1, so each norm is 1; the only integer solutions to a² + b² = 1 give these four numbers.

Which numbers are Gaussian primes?

A Gaussian prime is a nonzero Gaussian integer that is not a unit and cannot be factored into two nonunits. In ℤ[i], this agrees with the equivalent prime-divisibility definition.

An ordinary prime need not remain prime in the larger system. For example,

5 = (2 + i)(2 − i).

Both factors have norm 5, so neither is a unit. Therefore 5 is not a Gaussian prime. But 2 + i is a Gaussian prime: if 2 + i = uv, then 5 = N(u)N(v). One norm must be 1, making that factor a unit.

The ordinary prime 3 remains prime in ℤ[i]. A factorization of 3 into two nonunits would require both norms to equal 3, since N(3) = 9. Yet a² + b² = 3 has no integer solutions: squares no larger than 3 are only 0 and 1.

The Pythagorean formula appears again

Square a Gaussian integer:

(m + ni)² = (m² − n²) + 2mn i.

Taking norms gives

(m² − n²)² + (2mn)² = (m² + n²)².

These are exactly the expressions in Euclid’s formula. For instance, (2 + i)² = 3 + 4i, and taking norms gives 3² + 4² = 5². Squaring a Gaussian integer produces the legs of a Pythagorean triple as its real and imaginary parts.

We now have infinitely many solutions for exponent 2 and a formula describing them. What happens when we replace 2 by a larger exponent?

8. Fermat’s Last Theorem

A Diophantine equation is an equation for which we seek integer solutions. Pythagorean triples solve one such equation.

Fermat’s Last Theorem. For every integer n > 2, the equation

xn + yn = zn

has no positive integer solutions.

The word “positive” matters: allowing zero would give 0n + 1n = 1n. This theorem is also different from Fermat’s little theorem about congruences, encountered earlier.

Why prime exponents are enough

If an odd prime p divides n, write n = pk. A solution for exponent n would give

(xk)p + (yk)p = (zk)p,

a solution for exponent p. If n > 2 has no odd prime divisor, it is a power of 2 and is divisible by 4. A solution then produces one for exponent 4. Therefore it suffices to handle exponent 4 and every odd prime exponent.

9. Sophie Germain’s strategy

For an odd prime exponent p, mathematicians distinguished two cases. In the first case, p divides none of x, y, z. In the second case, p divides at least one variable.

Sophie Germain investigated Fermat’s equation using auxiliary primes: additional primes chosen to impose useful restrictions on its remainders. A celebrated consequence of her work is the following.

If p is an odd prime and 2p + 1 is also prime, then the first case of Fermat’s Last Theorem holds for exponent p.

A prime p for which 2p + 1 is prime is now called a Sophie Germain prime. Examples are 3, 5, and 11, with auxiliary primes 7, 11, and 23. The result above does not by itself settle the second case.

How an auxiliary prime restricts a solution

Take p = 5 and auxiliary prime 11. The fifth powers modulo 11 are:

a012345678910
a⁵ mod 110110111101010110

Since 10 ≡ −1 (mod 11), every fifth power is 0, 1, or −1 modulo 11. Suppose x⁵ + y⁵ = z⁵ and none of the variables is divisible by 11. The powers must then be 1 or −1. But adding two such values gives 2, 0, or −2, never 1 or −1 modulo 11. This is impossible.

Thus any hypothetical solution must have a variable divisible by 11. This illustrates the auxiliary-prime method; it is not the complete proof of Germain’s theorem, which concerns divisibility by the exponent prime 5. Further arguments turn modular restrictions into stronger divisibility conclusions. Germain’s broader plan sought enough such restrictions to rule out solutions altogether. [2]

10. What is an elliptic curve?

To understand the route to Wiles’s proof, we need a new mathematical object. For our purposes, an elliptic curve over the rational numbers can be represented by

y² = x³ + Ax + B,   4A³ + 27B² ≠ 0,

where A and B are rational numbers, together with a distinguished point at infinity, denoted O. A rational point is a solution whose two coordinates are rational.

The condition ensures that the curve is nonsingular: it has no singularity such as a cusp or a crossing of branches. For example,

E: y² = x³ − x + 1

is an elliptic curve because 4(−1)³ + 27(1)² = 23 ≠ 0. It contains (0, 1), (1, 1), and (−1, 1). By contrast, y² = x³ has a cusp at the origin and is not an elliptic curve. Despite its name, an elliptic curve is not an ellipse.

Adding points

Elliptic curves have an operation called addition of points. For distinct points P and Q, draw the line through them. In the ordinary case it meets the cubic at a third point R, counting multiplicity. Reflect R across the horizontal axis; the result is P + Q.

On y² = x³ − x + 1, take P = (0, 1) and Q = (1, 1). Their line is y = 1. Substitution gives

1 = x³ − x + 1,   x(x − 1)(x + 1) = 0.

The third intersection is R = (−1, 1). Reflecting it gives P + Q = (−1, −1).

To add a point to itself, use its tangent. A vertical line leads to the point at infinity, which acts as zero:

P + O = P,   P + (−P) = O.

The rational points form an abelian group: the operation stays within the set, is associative and commutative, has an identity, and gives every point an inverse. Associativity requires a proof; it is not immediate from the picture. The curve is both a geometric object and an arithmetic system.

11. What does “modular” mean?

A complex number is z = u + iv, with real u and v and i² = −1. Its real part is u and its imaginary part is v. The upper half-plane is the set with v > 0.

A modular form is a complex-valued function on the upper half-plane satisfying precise analytic and symmetry conditions. It is holomorphic—complex differentiable at every point—obeys transformation rules, and satisfies regularity conditions at boundary points called cusps.

For example, a modular form of integer weight k for the full modular group obeys

f((az + b)/(cz + d)) = (cz + d)k f(z)

for integers a, b, c, d with ad − bc = 1, together with the analytic and boundary conditions. Weight names the exponent in this rule. The forms associated with elliptic curves have weight 2 and use particular subgroups of these transformations.

These forms have expansions in powers of q = e2πiz. Here π is the circle constant. The coefficients can encode arithmetic information about a curve.

Counting points modulo a prime

For a prime p, the symbol 𝔽p denotes arithmetic with remainders modulo p. This is a field: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by any nonzero class are possible. Every nonzero class has a multiplicative inverse.

Reduce an appropriate integral equation for the curve modulo p and count its solutions. At a prime where the reduced curve remains nonsingular, define

ap = p + 1 − #E(𝔽p).

The symbol # means “number of,” and the point count includes O. For E: y² = x³ − x + 1 modulo 3, the right side equals 1 for all three values of x. Each has two possible values of y, namely 1 and 2. Including O gives 7 points, so a₃ = 3 + 1 − 7 = −3. The curve is nonsingular modulo 3.

For a modular elliptic curve, these ap agree with the coefficients of qp in a suitable normalized modular form. A geometric equation and a complex function thus carry matching arithmetic information. The technical definition requires specifying the form and its level, but this coefficient correspondence explains what the connection accomplishes.

12. Andrew Wiles and the contradiction

Suppose there were a counterexample ap + bp = cp for a prime p ≥ 5. One can associate a Frey curve of the form

y² = x(x − ap)(x + bp).

Its right side is a cubic. A change of coordinates over the rationals can put it into the form used above. After normalizing a hypothetical counterexample, the associated curve is semistable. Roughly, at each prime an appropriate reduced model is either nonsingular or has only a mild singularity called a node, rather than a cusp.

  1. A Fermat counterexample would produce this special semistable elliptic curve.
  2. Ribet’s theorem, building on Frey’s and Serre’s work, implies that this curve could not be modular.
  3. Wiles proved that every semistable elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular.

The same curve would have to be modular and nonmodular. Therefore the assumed counterexample cannot exist. Together with the already established exponents 3 and 4, this proves Fermat’s Last Theorem. [3]

Wiles announced his proof in 1993. A gap was found, and he and Richard Taylor resolved it in 1994. The proof and companion work appeared in 1995. [4]

This is the logical structure of the argument, not a proof of the modularity theorem. Establishing that theorem is the difficult step.

13. Hilbert’s list of problems

A conjecture is a mathematical statement proposed as true but not yet proved. A theorem has been established by proof.

In 1900, David Hilbert put forward a famous list of 23 problems to guide future mathematical research. His eighth problem concerned prime numbers and included the Riemann Hypothesis. [1]

Two other famous questions associated with that problem are Goldbach’s conjecture—every even integer greater than 2 is a sum of two primes—and the twin-prime conjecture—there are infinitely many prime pairs differing by 2.

For example, 20 = 3 + 17 = 7 + 13, and (11, 13) is a twin-prime pair. Examples illustrate a claim; a finite list cannot establish a universal statement or a claim of infinitude.

14. The Riemann zeta function

For a real number s > 1, define

ζ(s) = ∑n=1∞ 1/ns = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ···.

Thus ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + ···. An infinite series converges when its partial sums approach a finite limit. This series converges for real s > 1; at s = 1 it becomes the divergent harmonic series.

Why the function contains information about primes

For each prime p, the geometric-series identity gives

1/(1 − p−s) = 1 + p−s + p−2s + ···.

Multiplying these expressions over the primes generates terms such as 2−2s3−s = (2²·3)−s = 12−s. By unique prime factorization, every positive integer contributes exactly one term. We obtain Euler’s product:

ζ(s) = ∏p prime 1/(1 − p−s),   s > 1.

The symbol ∏ means a product, just as ∑ means a sum. Convergence justifies this infinite expansion. The sum runs over all positive integers; the product runs over primes. Unique factorization connects them.

15. The Riemann Hypothesis

The zeta series also converges for complex s with Re(s) > 1. Here Re(s) denotes the real part. Through analytic continuation, mathematicians extend the function beyond that region: the extension agrees with the original function wherever the original series converges. The extended zeta function is defined throughout the complex plane except for a pole at s = 1, where it becomes unbounded.

A zero of a function is an input where its value is zero. The extended zeta function has zeros at −2, −4, −6, …, called its trivial zeros. Its other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1.

Riemann Hypothesis. Every nontrivial zero of ζ(s) has real part 1/2.

Geometrically, all these zeros would lie on the vertical line Re(s) = 1/2 in the complex plane. The hypothesis remains unproved. [5]

The Prime Number Theorem describes the leading behavior of the prime count. The Riemann Hypothesis would give much tighter control over its fluctuations around a refined approximation. The remarkable connection is that the locations of zeros of a complex function govern the distribution of ordinary prime numbers.
