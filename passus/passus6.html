The road to Euclid

How can a collection of geometric discoveries become a mathematical theory? We have already encountered constructions and proofs associated with early Greek mathematics. Euclid’s Elements brings such results into an ordered argument: each new conclusion rests on definitions, stated assumptions, and results established earlier. Following that argument helps us see both the power of proof and the importance of asking exactly what a proof assumes.

The setting for this work developed over several generations. In Athens, Socrates encouraged the examination of claims through questions and reasoning. His student Plato founded the Academy around 387 BCE, creating a community in which mathematics and philosophy could be studied together. Aristotle spent about twenty years there before eventually establishing his own school. Their intellectual world valued explanations: knowing that a claim was true was different from understanding why it was true.

Later, Alexander’s conquests connected regions from the eastern Mediterranean and Egypt to Persia and parts of India. After his death in 323 BCE, his empire divided into kingdoms. Alexandria, founded in Egypt during his lifetime, became an important center of Greek learning under the Ptolemies. Euclid worked there around 300 BCE. We know little securely about his life, so his surviving mathematics gives us a firmer starting point than stories about his personality.

The Elements contains thirteen books, covering plane and solid geometry, ratios, and properties of numbers. Euclid drew on the work of earlier mathematicians. His achievement lay in organizing mathematics into a sustained deductive structure, as well as in the arguments themselves. The work shaped mathematical education for more than two thousand years.

We will follow the beginning and the end of Book I: first its definitions and assumptions, then the construction of an equilateral triangle, and finally its proof of the Pythagorean theorem. We will then step onto a sphere and ask which familiar geometric claims survive.

The language of geometry

Before proving anything, Euclid must tell us what his words mean. Book I begins with twenty-three definitions. Some sound familiar; others reveal how differently geometry can be expressed without coordinates or algebra.

Points and lines

A point has position but no size. A line has length but no breadth. In Euclid’s terminology, a line need not be straight: a curved boundary is also a line. A straight line is a special kind of line. These descriptions direct our imagination toward ideal objects. A pencil mark has thickness, but the mathematical line it represents does not.

Modern axiomatic treatments often leave words such as point and line undefined and specify how those objects behave through axioms. Euclid instead begins with descriptions that appeal to geometric understanding. Neither approach turns a physical drawing into the exact object of a proof.

Right angles and circles

Imagine one straight line standing on another. If the two adjacent angles are equal, each is a right angle, and the lines are perpendicular. Euclid thus describes a right angle through equality, rather than by assigning it the number 90. He also uses acute and obtuse angles; what he does not use is our routine numerical measurement of angles in degrees. A straight angle is described as two right angles.

Euclid’s circle is the plane figure enclosed by a boundary whose points are all equally distant from a point inside, the center. In modern terminology we usually distinguish the circle itself from the disk it bounds. The equal distances from the center are its radii. This equality, rather than the appearance of roundness, will do the work in our first proof.

Parallel lines and congruent figures

Parallel straight lines lie in the same plane and never meet, however far they are extended in either direction. The requirement that they lie in one plane matters: two lines in space may fail to meet without being parallel.

We call two figures congruent when one can be moved onto the other exactly by translations, rotations, or reflections, without changing its size or shape. Congruent triangles have corresponding sides and angles equal. Equal area is a different claim: a square and a long thin rectangle can have the same area without being congruent. This distinction will matter in Euclid’s proof of the Pythagorean theorem.

What we are allowed to assume

Definitions explain terms; postulates authorize constructions or state geometric assumptions. Euclid’s first three postulates describe the basic operations behind straightedge-and-compass construction. In modern wording, his five postulates are as follows.

1. A straight segment can be drawn joining any two distinct points.

2. A straight segment can be extended continuously along the same straight line.

3. A circle can be drawn with a given center and radius.

4. All right angles are equal.

5. If a transversal crosses two coplanar straight lines and the interior angles on one side add to less than two right angles, those lines, extended, meet on that side.

A straightedge draws and extends lines; it is not a marked ruler used to read lengths. A compass draws circles. Euclid does not initially take for granted every operation we perform with a modern compass: Proposition I.2 constructs a segment equal to a given segment at a specified point, and I.3 cuts off a prescribed shorter length from a longer segment.

The fifth postulate is noticeably more complicated. Picture two lines crossed by a third. If the two inward-facing angles on one side total less than 180°, the postulate says that the lines must eventually meet on that side. It makes a claim about the indefinite extension of lines, beyond anything a drawing can show.

A familiar equivalent formulation in ordinary Euclidean geometry is this: through a point outside a line there is exactly one line parallel to it. The equivalence uses the surrounding assumptions of plane geometry; it is not simply a change of wording that we can carry into every possible geometric setting.

The common notions

Euclid also states principles used to compare magnitudes. They apply more broadly than the geometric constructions of the postulates.

1. Quantities equal to the same quantity are equal to one another.

2. Adding equal quantities to equal quantities gives equal totals.

3. Subtracting equal quantities from equal quantities gives equal remainders.

4. Things that coincide are equal.

5. A whole is greater than a proper part.

For example, if a = c and b = c, the first common notion gives a = b. Here the whole-and-part principle concerns ordinary geometric magnitudes. Infinite sets require a different discussion. Today we often use axiom and postulate interchangeably; Euclid’s separation emphasizes the roles these assumptions play in his argument.

Constructing an equilateral triangle

Book I opens with a task: given a segment AB, construct an equilateral triangle on it. The drawing may already be familiar, but Euclid asks us to justify both the construction and its result.

Draw a circle centered at A with radius AB. Draw a second circle centered at B with radius BA. Choose one of their intersection points and call it C. Join A to C and B to C. The circle constructions use Postulate 3, and the new segments use Postulate 1.

Because A is the center of the first circle, AC = AB. Because B is the center of the second, BC = BA. Thus AC and BC are equal to the same segment AB; the first common notion gives AC = BC. All three sides of triangle ABC are equal, so the triangle is equilateral.

What the drawing quietly assumes

The proof uses a point C where the circles intersect. But which of the five postulates guarantees that the intersection exists? Postulate 3 permits us to draw the circles; it does not explicitly guarantee a shared point. Our picture makes the intersection seem unavoidable, yet a picture is not an additional axiom.

A modern treatment can supply an appropriate continuity principle to justify this step. The construction remains valid in the ordinary Euclidean plane. The issue is that Euclid’s stated foundations do not explicitly contain every assumption his reasoning uses. Identifying such a gap helps us distinguish a convincing diagram from a complete deductive justification.

Check your understanding: would choosing the other intersection of the circles change the conclusion? It would put the triangle on the opposite side of AB, but the same radius equalities prove that it too is equilateral.

Euclid proves the Pythagorean theorem

Explore the proof step by step →

Near the end of Book I, Proposition I.47 states that the square on the hypotenuse of a right triangle has the same area as the two squares on its legs together. We know the formula a² + b² = c². Euclid proves an equality of areas using congruent triangles and figures between parallel lines.

Let ABC have a right angle at A. Construct the outward squares ABFG, ACKH, and BCED. Draw AL parallel to BD and CE, meeting BC at M and DE at L. This divides the large square into rectangles BMLD and MCEL. We will show that each rectangle has the area of one of the smaller squares.

The tool we need is a result proved earlier in Book I: a triangle has half the area of a parallelogram on the same base and between the same parallel lines. In modern language, their base and perpendicular height agree. Sliding the triangle’s opposite vertex along a parallel line changes its shape but does not change its area.

Following the area argument

The square on AB and the first rectangle

Compare triangles ABD and FBC in the figure above. We have AB = FB because they are sides of one square, and BD = BC because they are sides of another. The included angles ABD and FBC are equal: each consists of the angle ABC together with a right angle. The triangles are therefore congruent by side–angle–side, and so their areas are equal.

Triangle ABD has base BD, and its vertex A lies on the line ML parallel to BD. Rectangle BMLD has the same base and lies between those same parallels. Its area is therefore twice the area of triangle ABD.

Triangle FBC has base FB. Since AB is perpendicular to both AC and AG, the points G, A, and C are collinear. The opposite vertex C therefore lies on the same line as the opposite side GA of square ABFG. The square and triangle share base FB and have the same height, so the square’s area is twice the area of triangle FBC.

The two triangles have equal areas. Doubling those equal areas gives the equality we wanted: the area of rectangle BMLD equals the area of square ABFG.

The square on AC and the second rectangle

Now join AE and BK. Compare triangles ACE and BCK. Their corresponding sides satisfy AC = CK and CE = CB, while the included angles ACE and KCB each equal a right angle plus angle ACB. Side–angle–side again gives congruent triangles.

Rectangle MCEL has twice the area of triangle ACE, using base CE and the parallel line AL. Square ACKH has twice the area of triangle BCK, using base CK and the line through H, A, and B. Consequently, rectangle MCEL has the same area as square ACKH.

The two rectangles exactly fill the square on BC. Adding their areas proves that the square on BC equals the squares on AB and AC together. This is the Pythagorean theorem. The triangles used in the proof are congruent; the rectangles and squares being compared need only have equal areas.

The converse

Proposition I.48 reverses the implication. Suppose a triangle has side lengths a, b, and c, with a² + b² = c². Construct a right triangle with legs a and b. By I.47 its hypotenuse d satisfies d² = a² + b² = c², so d = c. The two triangles have the same three side lengths and are congruent by side–side–side. The angle opposite c in the original triangle must therefore be right.

Geometry on the surface of a sphere

Now imagine that our entire geometric world is the surface of a sphere. We may travel along the surface, but we may not take a shortcut through its interior. To discuss lines and distances in this world, we must first say what those words mean.

A great circle is the intersection of the sphere with a plane through its center. The equator is a great circle; a full meridian, continued around the other side of the globe, is another. A circle of latitude other than the equator is not a great circle. Great circles serve as the lines of spherical geometry. Although curved in the surrounding space, their arcs are locally straight paths on the surface, called geodesics.

For two points that are not opposite one another, the shorter great-circle arc is the unique shortest surface path between them. Opposite points, called antipodal points, have infinitely many shortest paths: the North and South Poles, for example, can be joined along any meridian. A complete great circle can be followed indefinitely, but it closes after one circuit. An arc longer than half a circuit is no longer a shortest path between its endpoints.

Two distinct great circles always intersect at two antipodal points. Their defining planes pass through the sphere’s center and meet along a line through that center; the ends of that line on the sphere are the intersection points. Thus there are no parallel great circles.

The angle between two arcs is measured between their tangent directions at the vertex. In triangle NPQ, each meridian meets the equator at a right angle, and the two meridians meet at N at a right angle. The angle sum is therefore 90° + 90° + 90° = 270°.

For a nondegenerate convex spherical triangle bounded by shorter great-circle arcs, the angle sum exceeds 180°. Very small triangles approximate plane triangles, so their excess can be tiny. The familiar plane proof uses a parallel through a vertex to transfer angles. On the sphere that parallel cannot be drawn.

Which postulates survive on the sphere

The answer depends on keeping our definitions precise: points lie on a sphere of radius R, lines are great circles, and distance is the length of a shortest surface path. We should not label every postulate simply true or false without examining what it says.

Joining points and extending lines

Postulate 1 survives as an existence statement: any two points can be joined by a great-circle arc. Uniqueness needs qualification. Nonantipodal points determine one great circle and one shorter arc; antipodal points determine infinitely many great circles. The familiar plane assertion that two points determine a unique line therefore fails for antipodal points.

Postulate 2 survives as continued extension along a geodesic: we can keep following a great circle. But extension eventually returns to the starting point. It does not produce an unbounded, nonrepeating line, and it does not preserve global shortest distance as the arc grows.

Circles and right angles

For Postulate 3, use surface distance to define a circle: all points at distance r from a chosen center. For 0 < r < πR, this gives a circle on the sphere. At r = πR it collapses to the single antipodal point; for r > πR there are no points at that distance. Thus circles exist for an appropriate range of radii, but an unrestricted plane version allowing every positive radius does not carry over.

Postulate 4 survives. A right angle is still one quarter of a full turn in the tangent plane at a point, and right angles have the same size everywhere on the sphere.

The parallel postulate needs special care

The familiar unique-parallel formulation fails: through a point outside a great circle there are no great circles that avoid it. Euclid’s original wording about interior angles, extension, and meeting on a specified side is harder to transport because closed great circles do not behave like ordered, unbounded plane lines. Its usual equivalence with the unique-parallel formulation relies on other plane assumptions.

The sphere is therefore not a model obtained by preserving every other Euclidean assumption and changing only the fifth. It changes global incidence and extension as well. What it makes especially clear is that theorems depend on the space and assumptions under discussion. A triangle with three right angles is possible on a sphere without contradicting Euclid’s theorem about triangles in a plane.

Questions to carry forward

Euclid’s method asks us to separate three things: the objects we define, the assumptions we accept, and the conclusions we prove. The equilateral-triangle construction shows why even an obvious-looking intersection deserves attention. The area proof of the Pythagorean theorem shows how earlier results support a later one. The sphere shows why changing the setting can change the conclusions.

A guided comparison

Take the spherical triangle NPQ from the diagram. If the sphere has radius R, each side is a quarter of a great circle, so all three side lengths equal πR/2. Does the plane Pythagorean formula hold at one of its right angles?

Guidance: substitute the common side length s into the proposed equality. The two sides adjacent to a right angle would give s² + s², while the opposite side gives s². Since s > 0, these are unequal. A spherical right triangle does not generally satisfy the plane Pythagorean theorem. Calling an angle right does not import all the other assumptions of Euclidean geometry.

For discussion: in the equilateral-triangle construction, where do we use a definition, where do we use a postulate, and where do we use a common notion? In the Pythagorean proof, which figures are congruent and which merely have equal areas? On the sphere, why are ordinary latitude circles unsuitable as straight lines?

The postulate that would not become a theorem

Euclid’s fifth postulate looks different from the first four. Drawing a line between two points or a circle with a given center and radius seems straightforward. The fifth postulate asks us to consider two lines cut by a transversal and to predict where the lines will meet if extended. Its length and complexity made it look like something that ought to be proved.

The suspicion was strengthened by the structure of Book I. Euclid develops his first twenty-eight propositions without using the fifth postulate. He first invokes it in Proposition I.29, which establishes angle relationships when a transversal crosses parallel lines. Much of the geometry that follows depends on this result—including the results about squares and areas used in his Pythagorean proof.

For centuries, mathematicians tried to remove the fifth postulate from the list of assumptions and establish it as a theorem. The challenge was precise: prove it using the other assumptions alone. An argument that quietly used an equivalent statement would not solve the problem, however convincing its diagram appeared.

Centuries of attempted proofs

The problem attracted mathematicians across languages and cultures. The ancient commentator Proclus discussed it; later, mathematicians working in the Islamic world, including Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, examined the foundations of parallel-line theory. Their investigations helped expose how much familiar geometric reasoning depends on assumptions that can easily pass unnoticed.

One tempting approach was to replace the troublesome postulate with a more intuitive claim. John Wallis, for example, used the possibility of constructing similar triangles at different sizes. We readily imagine enlarging a triangle while preserving its angles. But that possibility is itself tied to the parallel postulate. A more familiar assumption is not necessarily a weaker one.

Another strategy was proof by contradiction. Keep the other assumptions, suppose that the expected conclusion fails, and try to derive an impossibility. This approach brought mathematicians remarkably close to a new geometry.

Saccheri and the quadrilateral that refused to be a rectangle

In 1733, the Italian mathematician Girolamo Saccheri published a work intended to defend Euclid. Begin with a segment AB. At its endpoints, erect equal perpendicular segments AD and BC on the same side of AB, and join D to C. The angles at A and B are right angles, and AD = BC.

On ordinary paper the result looks like a rectangle. But we must not assume that its upper angles are right: that is precisely what is in question. Using congruent triangles, one can prove that the two upper angles are equal without using the parallel postulate. Saccheri then considered three possibilities: both are right, both are obtuse, or both are acute.

He ruled out the obtuse case within his framework, which included assumptions about unbounded lines. The acute case resisted him. He derived many unfamiliar consequences but failed to obtain a valid contradiction. Results that seemed unacceptable from a Euclidean viewpoint would later become part of hyperbolic geometry.

Johann Heinrich Lambert pursued related investigations in the eighteenth century. Adrien-Marie Legendre also devoted considerable effort to parallel-line theory. The repeated difficulty taught a crucial lesson: a conclusion can conflict with our geometric expectations without conflicting with logic.

What an equivalent formulation means

Two statements are equivalent when each can be proved from the other, using the remaining agreed assumptions. Equivalence does not mean that either statement can be proved without an additional assumption.

The qualification about the remaining assumptions matters. Here we work within the usual framework of neutral plane geometry: the standard assumptions about incidence, order, congruence, and continuity, but without a parallel postulate. Euclid’s original five-item list does not explicitly state all of this framework.

Within that setting, the following are equivalent formulations of the Euclidean parallel postulate:

FormulationWhat it asserts
Playfair’s axiomThrough a point outside a line, there is exactly one line that does not meet the given line.
Triangle angle sumEvery triangle has interior angles whose sum is two right angles, or 180°.
Existence of a rectangleThere exists a quadrilateral with four right angles.
Parallel-line angle ruleA transversal crossing two parallel lines makes equal alternate interior angles.
Similarity at different sizesThere exist triangles with equal corresponding angles that are not congruent.

We must be careful with the direction of the parallel-line angle rule. Euclid proves in I.27 that equal alternate interior angles imply that the lines do not meet, without using the fifth postulate. The converse—nonintersecting lines imply equal alternate interior angles—is the result in I.29 that requires it.

The triangle angle-sum theorem gives a familiar example of the dependence. Draw through one vertex a parallel to the opposite side. The parallel-line angle rule transfers the other two angles to this vertex, where the three angles together form a straight angle. If we used this angle-sum theorem to prove the fifth postulate, while relying on that same proof of the theorem, our reasoning would be circular.

The rectangle formulation is especially relevant to our Pythagorean proof. We built squares and divided the hypotenuse square into rectangles. The existence and properties of those figures already carry information supplied by the parallel postulate.

From failed proofs to a new geometry

In the nineteenth century, Nikolai Lobachevsky and János Bolyai independently developed a geometry in which the Euclidean parallel rule does not hold. Lobachevsky presented his ideas in 1826 and published them beginning in 1829; Bolyai’s account appeared in 1832. Carl Friedrich Gauss had also investigated these possibilities but did not publish a systematic account.

The change was not merely a new attempted proof. Mathematicians began studying the alternative on its own terms. Instead of asking only where it would break down, they asked what could be proved within it.

This geometry is called hyperbolic geometry. Through a point outside a line, infinitely many distinct lines fail to meet the given line. Here “parallel” is being used broadly to mean nonintersecting; more specialized terminology distinguishes different kinds of nonintersecting hyperbolic lines.

In the hyperbolic plane, every nondegenerate triangle has angle sum less than 180°. There are no rectangles with four right angles. Triangles with equal corresponding angles are congruent: their angles determine their size as well as their shape. Thus our familiar freedom to enlarge a figure without changing its angles is lost.

These are consequences of the new geometry, not contradictions. A contradiction would require proving both a statement and its negation from the same assumptions. Proving a statement that differs from a Euclidean theorem does not do that, because the assumptions have changed.

How a model answers the question

Unsuccessful searches for a contradiction do not by themselves establish consistency. A stronger argument is to construct a model: an interpretation of points, lines, and distance in which the proposed axioms hold.

In the Poincaré disk model, points lie inside an ordinary circular disk. Hyperbolic lines are represented by diameters and circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles. The boundary is not part of the hyperbolic plane. Distance is measured by a special rule, so the disk’s apparent Euclidean size does not make the hyperbolic plane finite.

An arc that looks curved on our page can therefore represent a straight line in the modeled geometry. Its geometric role is determined by the model’s rules, not by how ink looks in the surrounding Euclidean picture.

Beltrami’s work in 1868 supplied models that made a relative consistency argument possible. If the Euclidean mathematics used to construct such a model is consistent, then hyperbolic geometry is consistent too. The fifth postulate cannot be a consequence of the remaining neutral assumptions: otherwise it would have to hold in every model of them, including a hyperbolic model where it fails.

This establishes independence, rather than the falsehood of Euclidean geometry. Different systems of assumptions support different geometries.

What if there are no parallels

The sphere offers another possibility. Taking great circles as lines, every pair of distinct lines intersects, so there are no parallels. Our triangle formed by two meridians and an equatorial arc has three right angles, giving an angle sum of 270°.

However, we cannot obtain spherical geometry simply by retaining every neutral-plane assumption and replacing “exactly one parallel” with “no parallels.” Neutral geometry already guarantees at least one nonintersecting line through an external point. To obtain a geometry with none, other assumptions must change as well.

On a sphere, two great circles meet twice, antipodal points lie on infinitely many great circles, and lines close up rather than extending without repetition. Elliptic geometry makes a related construction by treating each pair of antipodal points as a single point. This restores a unique line through two distinct points and a unique intersection for two distinct lines, but the global order properties still differ from those of the Euclidean plane.

Geometric settingLines through an external point that do not meet a given lineTriangle angle sum
Euclidean planeExactly one180°
Hyperbolic planeInfinitely manyLess than 180°
Sphere with great-circle linesNoneGreater than 180° for ordinary convex triangles bounded by shorter arcs

What became of Euclid’s theorem

The Pythagorean theorem remains true in Euclidean geometry. It does not generally hold, in its familiar form, for distances in curved geometries. Our spherical triangle provides a direct check: all three sides are equal, yet all three angles are right. If its side length is s, the Euclidean formula would demand s² + s² = s², which is impossible for s > 0.

There is no failure of Euclid’s proof. Its constructions and area comparisons belong to the Euclidean setting. On the sphere, the required squares and rectangles are unavailable; in hyperbolic geometry, four-right-angle rectangles are also impossible.

The long struggle over the fifth postulate changed the question mathematicians asked about geometry. They could now distinguish what follows logically from specified assumptions from which geometry best describes a physical situation. Euclid’s plane remains a powerful mathematical model. It is one model among several, and its theorems tell us exactly what its assumptions allow us to conclude.
