Exam 1 Review

Exam 1 is 90 minutes, closed notes, and without calculators. Show your reasoning, label diagrams, and explain why your methods work. Review both problem sets and the relevant class discussions.

These problems illustrate the kinds of reasoning you should practice. They are not a list of the exact questions on the exam. This review is longer than the exam and may be completed across several study sessions.

Try each problem before opening its answer check. The checks do not replace the requested explanations, proofs, or diagrams.

1. Grouping systems and positional systems

In a simple grouping system, the symbols A, B, C have values 1, 5, 25.
  1. Express 68 using these symbols.
  2. Explain why CBA and ABC represent the same number in this system.
  3. Compare (231)5 and (132)5. Why does the order matter here?
  4. Explain the role of zero in (203)5. What changes if the zero is deleted?
Answer check — Problem 1

(a) Two C’s, three B’s, and three A’s: 2 · 25 + 3 · 5 + 3 = 68. (b) Each symbol has a fixed value, regardless of position. (c) The values are 66 and 42; position determines the power of 5 multiplying each digit. (d) (203)5 = 53, whereas (23)5 = 13. Zero holds the empty fives place.

2. Working with unfamiliar bases

  1. Convert (314)5 to base 10 and then to base 6.
  2. Calculate (45)6 + (34)6. Explain every carry.
  3. In an unknown integer base b, the numeral 21 represents the decimal number 15. Determine b.
  4. A student says that (28)7 represents 22 because 2 · 7 + 8 = 22. Explain what is wrong with this statement and write 22 correctly in base 7.
Answer check — Problem 2

(a) 84 = (220)6. (b) (123)6: 5 + 4 = 9 gives digit 3 and a carry of 1; 4 + 3 + 1 = 8 gives digit 2 and another carry of 1. (c) 2b + 1 = 15, so b = 7. (d) A base-7 digit must be between 0 and 6; 22 = (31)7.

3. Sexagesimal notation and empty places

Use a comma between integer places and a semicolon before fractional places.
  1. Convert (1,12;45)60 to base 10.
  2. Write 90¼ in base 60.
  3. Calculate (0;40)60 + (0;35)60.
  4. Compare (4,2)60 and (4,0,2)60. Explain why an omitted empty-place marker could cause a substantial error.
Answer check — Problem 3

(a) 72¾. (b) (1,30;15)60. (c) (1;15)60. (d) 242 and 14,402. Omitting the empty place changes which power of 60 multiplies the 4.

4. Egyptian multiplication and division

  1. Use a doubling table to calculate 29 × 12.
  2. Use the same table to calculate 300 ÷ 12.
  3. A student proposes using only rows labeled 1, 2, 4, 8, …, even when the desired multiplier is odd. Explain why this is sufficient.
  4. Explain why the method gives the correct product. Identify where the distributive law is used.
Answer check — Problem 4

(a) 348, selecting 16 + 8 + 4 + 1. (b) 25, selecting 16 + 8 + 1, whose multiples are 192 + 96 + 12 = 300. (c) Every positive integer is a sum of distinct powers of 2; an odd multiplier includes the row labeled 1. (d) The distributive law gives (16 + 8 + 4 + 1) · 12 = 16 · 12 + 8 · 12 + 4 · 12 + 1 · 12.

5. Unit fractions

A unit fraction has numerator 1 and a positive integer denominator.
  1. Verify that 1/n = 1/(n + 2) + 2/[n(n + 2)] for every positive integer n.
  2. When n is even, explain why the second term can be written as a unit fraction. Use this observation to write 1/6 as a sum of two distinct unit fractions.
  3. Find another representation of 1/6 as a sum of two distinct unit fractions.
  4. A student writes 2/5 = 1/5 + 1/5. The equality is correct. Why does it fail a requirement that the unit fractions be distinct? Find a representation satisfying that requirement.
Answer check — Problem 5

(a) The right side is (n + 2)/[n(n + 2)] = 1/n. (b) 2/[n(n + 2)] = 1/[n(n + 2)/2], whose denominator is an integer when n is even. Thus 1/6 = 1/8 + 1/24. (c) One possibility is 1/7 + 1/42. (d) The fraction 1/5 is repeated; one valid alternative is 1/3 + 1/15.

6. Finite representations of fractions

A fraction has a finite representation in base b if it can be written as m/bk for integers m and k ≥ 0.
  1. Which of 1/9, 1/16, and 1/14 have finite base-60 representations? Explain each answer.
  2. Give a fraction that terminates in base 60 but does not terminate in base 10.
  3. A student claims, “If the denominator is smaller than the base, the fraction terminates.” Give a counterexample.
  4. Explain why a reduced fraction whose denominator contains a prime factor other than 2, 3, or 5 cannot have a finite base-60 representation.
Answer check — Problem 6

(a) 1/9 and 1/16 terminate because their denominators divide 60²; 1/14 does not because of its factor 7. (b) For example, 1/3. (c) For example, 1/7 in base 10. (d) The only prime factors of any power of 60 are 2, 3, and 5. A reduced denominator containing another prime cannot divide a power of 60.

7. Similarity in a right triangle

Triangle ABC has a right angle at C. The altitude CD meets hypotenuse AB at D. Suppose AC = 6, BC = 8, and AB = 10.
  1. Draw the figure and identify three pairs of equal angles that establish the similarities among the three triangles.
  2. Use similarity to find AD and DB. Do not simply quote a formula for these segments.
  3. Find CD by calculating the area of triangle ABC in two ways.
  4. Explain why AD + DB = AB provides a useful check but does not, by itself, establish that your individual values are correct.
Answer check — Problem 7

(a) Each small triangle has a right angle at D, matching the original right angle at C. Angle CAD equals angle CAB, and angle CBD equals angle CBA. These give the similarities by AA. (b) AD/AC = AC/AB gives AD = 18/5; DB/BC = BC/AB gives DB = 32/5. (c) (6 · 8)/2 = (10 · CD)/2, so CD = 24/5. (d) Many incorrect pairs also sum to 10; the check is necessary but not sufficient.

8. Thales’ theorem in a new setting

A circle has center O and diameter AB. A point P lies on the circle, distinct from A and B. Suppose AP = 5 and BP = 12.
  1. Find AB and the radius of the circle. State the theorem that allows you to begin.
  2. Reflect P through O, obtaining a point Q. Explain why quadrilateral APBQ is a rectangle.
  3. Find the area of this rectangle.
  4. A student draws one such figure and concludes that the rectangle argument works for every position of P. What general facts must the student explain to justify that conclusion?
Answer check — Problem 8

(a) Thales’ theorem makes angle APB a right angle. The Pythagorean theorem gives AB = 13, so the radius is 13/2. (b) Diagonals AB and PQ bisect one another and have equal lengths, so the quadrilateral is a rectangle. (c) Area 60. (d) Reflection through O always produces another point of the circle; AB and PQ are always diameters with common midpoint O. These facts hold for every permitted P, not just the drawing.

9. Squares, remainders, and integer triangles

  1. By considering even and odd integers, prove that every integer square has remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 4.
  2. Prove that an integer right triangle cannot have both legs odd.
  3. Suppose an integer right triangle has an even hypotenuse. Explain why dividing all three side lengths by 2 produces another integer right triangle.
  4. A student claims, “Every Pythagorean triple contains an odd number.” Decide whether the claim is correct and justify your answer.
  5. Extension. Show that a square has remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 3. Deduce that at least one leg of an integer right triangle is divisible by 3.
Answer check — Problem 9

(a) (2k)² = 4k² and (2k + 1)² = 4(k² + k) + 1. (b) Two odd squares would sum to 2 modulo 4, which cannot be a square. (c) An even hypotenuse forces both legs to be even: only 0 + 0 among the possible square remainders sums to 0 modulo 4. Dividing the Pythagorean equation by 4 verifies the new triple. (d) False: (6, 8, 10). (e) Squaring the possible remainders 0, 1, 2 modulo 3 yields 0, 1, 1. If neither leg were divisible by 3, their squares would sum to 2 modulo 3, which cannot be a square.

10. Triangular arrays and sums

Let tn = 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n. You may use tn = n(n + 1)/2.
  1. Given t25 = 325, find t24 and t26 without using the formula.
  2. Remove the three longest rows of a triangular array with n rows (n ≥ 3). How many dots have been removed? Give an expression in terms of n.
  3. Two triangular arrays differ by three rows. Their numbers of dots differ by 72. Find the number of rows in each array.
  4. Calculate 16 + 17 + ⋯ + 35 as a difference of triangular numbers.
  5. Explain geometrically and algebraically why tn + tn−1 = n² (n ≥ 2).
Answer check — Problem 10

(a) 300 and 351. (b) n + (n − 1) + (n − 2) = 3n − 3. (c) 3n − 3 = 72 gives 25 and 22 rows. (d) t35 − t15 = 630 − 120 = 510. (e) Two consecutive triangular arrays combine into an n × n square: their row lengths can be paired as 1 + (n − 1), 2 + (n − 2), …, n + 0. Algebraically, n(n + 1)/2 + (n − 1)n/2 = n².

11. Examples, conjectures, and proof

A student calculates 1 + 3 = 4, 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, and 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 and announces that the sum of the first n positive odd integers is n².
  1. Explain what the calculations establish and what they do not establish.
  2. Give a geometric proof by explaining how to enlarge an (n − 1) × (n − 1) square of dots into an n × n square.
  3. Explain why your argument applies to every positive integer n, including the starting case.
Answer check — Problem 11

(a) The calculations verify three cases, not all positive integers. (b) Enlarging the square adds n dots along one side and n − 1 along the other, for 2n − 1 new dots. (c) Begin with the one-dot square. Each successive odd number adds precisely the border needed for the next square, so the construction continues for any positive integer n.

12. Evaluating historical evidence

Consider this hypothetical situation:
An ancient document contains a worked numerical example. Several centuries later, an author attributes the underlying method to a particular mathematician. A modern scholar supplies a general proof of the method.
  1. What does each source contribute to our understanding?
  2. Does the worked example establish that its author possessed a general proof? Explain.
  3. What would you want to know about the later author before accepting the attribution?
  4. Why does a correct modern reconstruction not establish which proof an ancient mathematician actually used?
  5. Relate one of these issues to your reading of Aristotle or Proclus.
Answer check — Problem 12

(a–b) Distinguish evidence of a worked example, a later attribution, and a general proof. A successful numerical example does not establish possession of a general proof. (c) Consider the later writer’s sources, distance from the events, purpose, and independent corroboration. (d) A reconstruction establishes that an argument is possible, not that someone historically used it. (e) Answers should identify a specific claim from the assigned reading and assess the evidence for it.

13. Computation and mathematical evidence

A computer checks a claim for the first million positive integers and finds no counterexample.
  1. Has it proved the claim for every positive integer? Explain.
  2. What could a single verified counterexample establish?
  3. Explain the difference between testing whether one particular number is prime and establishing a statement about infinitely many numbers.
  4. In a distributed project such as GIMPS, why is independent verification of a reported result valuable?
Answer check — Problem 13

(a) A finite set of successful checks does not establish an unrestricted universal claim. (b) One verified counterexample disproves it. (c) A conclusive primality test concerns a particular number; proving a claim about infinitely many numbers requires an argument covering all the relevant numbers. (d) Independent verification helps detect computational errors and supports confidence in the reported result.
