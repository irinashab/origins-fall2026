13. Hilbert’s list of problems

A conjecture is a mathematical statement proposed as true but not yet proved. A theorem has been established by proof. The gap between the two can last for centuries, as Fermat’s Last Theorem shows.

In 1900, at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, David Hilbert delivered a famous address listing 23 problems that he believed would guide mathematical research into the new century. Hilbert’s eighth problem concerned prime numbers and included the Riemann Hypothesis. [1]

Most of Hilbert’s 23 problems have since been solved. A handful remain open, and the eighth is the most famous of them.

Two other well-known questions associated with the eighth problem are Goldbach’s conjecture — every even integer greater than 2 is a sum of two primes — and the twin-prime conjecture — there are infinitely many prime pairs differing by 2.

For example, 20 = 3 + 17 = 7 + 13, and (11, 13) is a twin-prime pair. Examples illustrate a claim; a finite list cannot establish a universal statement or a claim of infinitude. What makes both conjectures hard is the absence of a general method: no known technique decides whether a given additive pattern among the primes occurs infinitely often. Partial results exist — Chen’s theorem, the Zhang–Maynard–Tao work on bounded prime gaps — but the full conjectures remain open.

14. The Riemann zeta function

The tools we have assembled — primes, remainders, factorization in larger number systems — reach their deepest form in the study of a single function. For a real number s > 1, define

ζ(s) = ∑n=1∞ 1/ns = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ···.

Thus ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + ···. An infinite series converges when its partial sums approach a finite limit. This series converges for real s > 1.

At s = 1 it becomes the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ···, which diverges: its partial sums grow without bound, even though each term shrinks to zero. The standard grouping argument shows this: the terms from 1/(2k+1) to 1/2k+1 sum to more than 1/2, and there are infinitely many such groups.

Why the function contains information about primes

For each prime p, the geometric-series identity gives

1/(1 − p−s) = 1 + p−s + p−2s + ···.

Multiplying these expressions over the primes generates terms such as 2−2s3−s = (2²·3)−s = 12−s. By unique prime factorization, every positive integer contributes exactly one term. We obtain Euler’s product:

ζ(s) = ∏p prime 1/(1 − p−s),   s > 1.

The symbol ∏ means a product, just as ∑ means a sum. Convergence justifies this infinite expansion. The sum runs over all positive integers; the product runs over primes. Unique factorization connects them. This identity is the bridge between the additive structure of the integers and the multiplicative structure of the primes — and it is why a single function can encode everything we might want to know about how the primes are distributed.

15. The Riemann Hypothesis

We have arrived at the deepest open question about primes — the one Hilbert placed at the center of his eighth problem, and the one that governs how tightly we can control the prime-counting function π(N).

The zeta series also converges for complex s with Re(s) > 1. Here Re(s) denotes the real part. Through analytic continuation, mathematicians extend the function beyond that region: the extension agrees with the original function wherever the original series converges. The extended zeta function is defined throughout the complex plane except for a pole at s = 1, where it becomes unbounded. The pole at s = 1 is not an accident; it is the analytic shadow of the divergence of the harmonic series.

A zero of a function is an input where its value is zero. The extended zeta function has zeros at −2, −4, −6, …, called its trivial zeros. Its other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1.

Riemann Hypothesis. Every nontrivial zero of ζ(s) has real part 1/2.

Geometrically, all these zeros would lie on the vertical line Re(s) = 1/2 in the complex plane. The hypothesis remains unproved. [5]

The Prime Number Theorem describes the leading behavior of the prime count. The Riemann Hypothesis would give much tighter control over its fluctuations around a refined approximation. Concretely, it would imply that

|π(N) − Li(N)| < C √N log N

for some constant C, where Li(N) is the logarithmic integral ∫2N dt / log t — a better approximation than N / log N. Without the hypothesis, we can only bound the error much more weakly. The remarkable connection is that the locations of zeros of a complex function govern the distribution of ordinary prime numbers.

This is where the passus ends, but the story does not. We began with Euclid’s proof that the primes are infinite, moved through remainders and the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and saw how factorizations in larger number systems — the Gaussian integers, then the arithmetic of elliptic curves and modular forms — turned a question about whole-number equations into a question about complex functions. Wiles’s proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem was a victory of that method. The Riemann Hypothesis is the problem that remains.
