Research Project

The research project is an opportunity to explore a mathematical idea beyond what we can cover together in class. Your goal is not simply to report historical facts, but to understand the mathematics involved and help the rest of us understand what the idea is, where it came from, and why it matters.

The project consists of three parts: a proposal and preparation, a presentation, and an individual written report.

Working with a Partner

Research projects and presentations will normally be completed in teams of two. You and your partner will choose a topic, conduct the research, and prepare and give the presentation together.

The written report, however, is individual. Each student will submit their own report after the presentation.

Choosing a Topic

Your topic should be connected to the development of mathematics in some meaningful way. Rather than providing a long list of specific topics, here are some possible directions:

  • The development of a mathematical idea
  • A mathematical problem and its history
  • Mathematics in a particular culture or historical period
  • A mathematician or group of mathematicians, with the emphasis on their mathematics rather than biography
  • A mathematical controversy, paradox, or failed idea
  • A surprising connection between mathematics and another field
  • A topic beyond the course that grew out of something we studied

This is absolutely not an exhaustive list. If you find something that interests you, propose it. Topics must be approved before you begin the full project.

Whatever topic you choose, there must be some mathematics to explain. A presentation about a mathematician, for example, should not simply tell us the story of that person's life; it should help us understand some of the mathematics associated with them.

Proposal and Preparation — 5%

Your team will make an initial project post in the Brightspace discussion. The post should include:

  • The names of both team members
  • Your proposed topic or central question
  • A brief explanation of why you think the topic is worth investigating
  • The mathematics you expect to explore or explain
  • Several preliminary reliable sources
  • Any presentation dates on which either team member is unavailable; you do not need to explain why

The initial post is a starting point, not a contract. Your project may change as you learn more about the subject. Your topic is considered approved after I respond to the proposal.

You will have an opportunity to discuss the project with me before the presentation.

Presentation — 15%

Presentations will begin around the middle of October and will become part of our regular course material rather than all taking place at the end of the semester.

The presentation should concentrate on the big picture. Give us enough historical and mathematical context to understand where the subject came from and why anyone became interested in it. Most importantly, teach us some mathematics.

Good examples are often more useful than large amounts of general information. You are not expected to tell us everything there is to know about your topic. Choose what is important and explain it well.

You should be prepared to answer reasonable questions about your topic after the presentation.

Presentation Rubric

The presentation will be evaluated in four areas, each on a 1–5 scale:

Criterion What I will look for
Context & Motivation Where did the idea come from? What problem or question motivated it? Why is it interesting or important?
Mathematical Content Does the presentation contain meaningful mathematics, with appropriate examples, arguments, calculations, or demonstrations?
Understanding Do the presenters demonstrate that they understand the mathematics and context they are explaining and can respond to questions?
Communication Is the presentation organized, clear, engaging, and understandable to the class?

5 — Excellent: thoughtful, accurate, substantial, and particularly effective

4 — Strong: clear and well developed, with only minor weaknesses

3 — Satisfactory: meets the basic expectations but could use greater clarity, development, or depth

2 — Limited: important elements are missing or insufficiently developed

1 — Inadequate: little evidence that the criterion has been addressed

Individual Report — 15%

After your presentation, each member of the team will submit an individual report.

The report is not simply a transcript of your presentation. It gives you an opportunity to develop the topic in greater depth, explain mathematics that you did not have time to include in the presentation, and incorporate ideas or questions that arose during the presentation and discussion.

Think of the report as expository writing: your purpose is to teach an interested reader something about your subject.

Your report should provide appropriate historical or intellectual context, explain the mathematics clearly, and use reliable sources. All factual and historical claims that are not common knowledge should be supported appropriately.

Sources and AI

Use reliable sources and keep track of them as you research. AI tools are not sources. Any factual or historical information obtained through an AI tool must be independently verified using sources you have actually consulted.

The course AI policy applies to every part of the research project: AI may help you explore ideas and understand unfamiliar concepts, but it may not generate your presentation or write, rewrite, or substantially edit your report. You should be able to explain and defend everything you present or submit.
