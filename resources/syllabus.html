Course Description

This course is not a history of mathematics. Rather, we will explore how some of the ideas that we now consider fundamental mathematics developed: how people learned to represent and work with numbers, why proof became central to mathematics, how geometry changed over time, why mathematicians needed new kinds of numbers, and how ideas such as infinity and calculus emerged.

Along the way we will meet mathematicians from different periods and cultures and consider mathematics as part of a broader human history. We will not simply learn what mathematicians discovered; whenever possible, we will do the mathematics ourselves.

Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of the course, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how important mathematical ideas developed in their historical and cultural contexts.
  2. Compare different approaches to numbers, calculation, geometry, and mathematical reasoning.
  3. Solve mathematical problems using methods developed in different periods and cultures.
  4. Construct, analyze, and communicate mathematical arguments.
  5. Trace the development of several major mathematical ideas, such as number, proof, geometry, infinity, complex numbers, and calculus.
  6. Research a mathematical topic independently and communicate the results effectively in writing and orally.

Problem Sets

Several problem sets will be assigned during the semester. Problems may involve calculations, proofs and mathematical arguments, historical methods, and conceptual questions. Some problems will be short; others may require substantial thought and exploration.

Afterthoughts

Short Afterthoughts will be given near the end of many class meetings. They may ask you to solve a small problem, explain an idea, make a conjecture, compare approaches, or respond to something discussed in class. Afterthoughts are low-stakes and are evaluated primarily for thoughtful engagement rather than perfect correctness or completion. Your three lowest Afterthought scores will be dropped.

Exams

There will be three exams during the semester. A cumulative final exam will be optional for most students. If you take the final, the final-exam score will replace your lowest semester-exam score, even if the final-exam score is lower.

I may require a student to take the final if their semester work does not provide sufficient evidence of individual understanding. A student who misses one of the three semester exams will be required to take the final.

Research project

The research project is a major component of the course and consists of three parts: a proposal and research preparation, an oral presentation, and an individual written report.

Research and presentations will normally be completed in teams of two. Presentations will begin during the second half of the semester and will become part of our regular course material. Each student will submit an individual report after the presentation.

Policy on ChatGPT and Large Language Models (LLMs)

Bottom line: you may use AI tools for curiosity and exploration, but not to generate or write your assignments.

The use of ChatGPT and other large language models is not allowed for writing assignments in this course, unless you receive explicit permission from me in advance. This class is about learning the material by engaging with it directly - struggling, questioning, and building understanding step by step. You should improve your problem-solving skills, practice in doing proofs, etc. Letting an AI generate your work short-circuits that learning process. Moreover, these tools often produce incorrect or misleading content. Permitted Uses: Exploratory Investigation. You may use LLMs like ChatGPT to explore broader questions, investigate ideas, or find sources—just not to generate or write your actual assignments. For example, questions like “What was the reason to introduce imaginary numbers?”or “What are quaternions?” are curiosity-driven and meant to spark learning and discovery, not solve assigned problems for you. If you're unsure whether something is allowed, just ask me—I'm happy to help clarify.
